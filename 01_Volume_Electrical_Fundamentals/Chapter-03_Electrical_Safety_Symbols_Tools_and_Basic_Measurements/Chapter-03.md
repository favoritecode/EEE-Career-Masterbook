\# Chapter 03

\# Electrical Safety, Symbols, Tools and Basic Measurements

\---

\## Introduction

Electrical Engineering requires more than understanding Voltage, Current and Resistance. Students and technicians must also recognize hazards, follow safe working procedures, understand electrical symbols, select suitable tools and use measuring instruments correctly.

Electricity is useful but can cause Electric Shock, burns, fire, Arc Flash, equipment damage and death when hazards are not controlled. Safe work begins with planning, isolation, verification and the use of correctly rated tools and protective equipment.

This chapter introduces Electrical Safety, common symbols, basic tools, measuring instruments, SI Units and practical safety rules in a beginner-friendly and engineering-oriented manner. It is intended for university students, diploma students, technical job preparation, competitive examinations and self-learning.

\---

\- Identify common electrical hazards and basic risk-control methods.
\- Describe the purpose and limitations of Personal Protective Equipment.
\- Recognize common electrical and electronic symbols.
\- Select basic tools for electrical work.
\- Explain the functions of common measuring instruments.
\- Connect an Ammeter and Voltmeter correctly.
\- Apply safe procedures when measuring Voltage, Current and Resistance.
\- Identify common electrical quantities, symbols and SI Units.
\- Apply practical safety rules in homes, laboratories and industries.

\## Topic 01: Introduction to Electrical Safety

\### Definition

Electrical Safety is the system of knowledge, procedures, equipment and behavior used to prevent injury, fire, equipment damage and other losses caused by electrical energy.

It includes:

\- Recognizing hazards before work begins
\- De-energizing equipment whenever practical
\- Preventing unexpected energization
\- Verifying the absence of Voltage
\- Using correctly rated tools and instruments
\- Maintaining safe distance from exposed energized parts
\- Using suitable PPE when required
\- Following applicable standards and authorized procedures

\---

\### Importance

Electrical Safety protects people, equipment and property. A small mistake, such as selecting the wrong meter range or touching a damaged conductor, can produce severe consequences.

Electrical Safety is important for:

\- Preventing Electric Shock and electrocution
\- Preventing electrical and thermal burns
\- Reducing Arc Flash and Arc Blast exposure
\- Preventing short circuits and fires
\- Protecting instruments and electrical equipment
\- Maintaining reliable industrial operation
\- Meeting workplace rules and legal requirements
\- Developing professional engineering discipline

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Hazard and Risk

A Hazard is a source with the potential to cause harm. Exposed energized conductors are an electrical Hazard.

Risk combines the likelihood of an incident with the severity of its possible consequences.

\#### 2. De-Energized Work

Electrical equipment should normally be de-energized before inspection, connection, repair or maintenance. Switching a control button OFF does not by itself prove that every hazardous energy source has been isolated.

\#### 3. Treat as Energized Until Verified

A circuit must be treated as energized until an authorized and competent person has isolated it and verified the absence of Voltage using suitable test equipment.

Back-feed, stored energy, induced Voltage, incorrect labeling and wiring faults may keep a circuit hazardous even after a switch is opened.

\#### 4. Qualified and Authorized Persons

Work on or near exposed energized parts requires appropriate training, authorization, tools, PPE and procedures. Beginners must not perform energized work merely because a Voltage appears low or a task appears simple.

\#### 5. Safety Hierarchy

The preferred approach is:

1\. Eliminate the electrical Hazard where possible.
2\. De-energize and isolate the energy source.
3\. Apply engineering controls such as barriers, insulation and protective devices.
4\. Apply administrative controls such as procedures, permits and training.
5\. Use PPE suitable for the remaining Risk.

PPE is important, but it does not replace proper isolation and safe work planning.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

Electrical Safety is applied when:

\- Replacing a household switch or socket
\- Testing a laboratory power supply
\- Maintaining a motor-control panel
\- Inspecting a distribution board
\- Connecting batteries or solar panels
\- Troubleshooting industrial machinery
\- Working near overhead power lines
\- Handling damaged cables and portable tools

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Electrical Safety protects people, equipment and property.
\- Hazard and Risk are related but different concepts.
\- De-energized work is normally the safest approach.
\- A circuit must be treated as energized until absence of Voltage is verified.
\- Only trained and authorized persons should perform hazardous electrical work.
\- PPE is the last layer of protection, not a substitute for isolation.

\## Topic 02: Electrical Hazards and Risk Prevention

\### Definition

An Electrical Hazard is any condition involving electrical energy that can cause Electric Shock, burns, fire, explosion, Arc Flash, equipment damage or another harmful event.

Risk Prevention means identifying those hazards and applying suitable controls before and during work.

\---

\### Importance

Electrical hazards are not always visible. A conductor may appear harmless while carrying dangerous Voltage, and a disconnected capacitor may retain stored energy.

Hazard recognition helps students and workers:

\- Avoid direct and indirect contact
\- Select suitable protective devices
\- Prevent incorrect instrument connections
\- Control stored and unexpected energy
\- Respond correctly to damaged equipment
\- Reduce fire and Arc Flash Risk

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Common Electrical Hazards

| Hazard | Typical Cause | Possible Result | Basic Prevention |
|---|---|---|---|
| Electric Shock | Contact with energized parts | Muscle injury, burns or death | Isolation, guarding, insulation and safe distance |
| Short Circuit | Unintended low-Resistance path | High Current, heat and equipment damage | Correct wiring and overcurrent protection |
| Overload | Current above equipment rating | Overheating and fire | Correct cable, load and protective-device rating |
| Earth Fault | Live conductor contacts exposed metal or Earth | Shock and fault Current | Earthing, bonding and suitable protective devices |
| Arc Flash | Electrical arc releases intense energy | Severe burns, light and pressure injury | De-energization, assessment, boundaries and suitable PPE |
| Fire | Loose connection, overload or insulation failure | Property damage and injury | Inspection, correct protection and maintenance |
| Stored Energy | Charged capacitor, battery or rotating equipment | Shock, arc or unexpected movement | Discharge, isolate and verify |

\#### 2. Direct and Indirect Contact

Direct Contact occurs when a person touches an energized conductor.

Indirect Contact occurs when a normally non-energized conductive part becomes energized because of an insulation or wiring fault.

\#### 3. Effects of Electric Current

The effect of Current on the human body depends on:

\- Current magnitude
\- Duration of contact
\- Current path through the body
\- AC or DC and Frequency
\- Skin condition and contact area
\- Environmental conditions

Wet skin usually lowers body Resistance and can increase Current for the same applied Voltage.

\#### 4. Lockout and Tagout

Lockout prevents an energy-isolating device from being operated. A Tag communicates the identity, reason and status of the isolation.

A basic controlled sequence is:

1\. Identify all energy sources.
2\. Shut down the equipment correctly.
3\. Isolate every relevant source.
4\. Apply approved locks and tags.
5\. Release or control stored energy.
6\. Verify that isolation is effective.

Only authorized procedures and personnel may apply or remove workplace locks and tags.

\#### 5. Protective Devices

Fuses and Circuit Breakers protect circuits against excessive Current. Residual-current protection can reduce certain shock risks by detecting Current imbalance.

No protective device makes unsafe contact acceptable. Correct installation, rating, testing and Earthing remain necessary.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

Hazard prevention includes:

\- Replacing cracked plugs and damaged cables
\- Keeping electrical equipment away from water
\- Preventing extension-board overload
\- Tightening connections with power isolated
\- Locking a motor isolator during maintenance
\- Discharging capacitors using an approved method
\- Using barriers around energized industrial equipment
\- Reporting burning smell, sparks or abnormal heating immediately

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Electrical hazards include Shock, Arc Flash, fire, overload and stored energy.
\- Direct Contact and Indirect Contact require different protective measures.
\- Human injury depends on Current, time, path and environment.
\- Lockout and Tagout help prevent unexpected energization.
\- Protective devices reduce Risk but do not replace safe behavior.
\- Wet conditions significantly increase electrical danger.

\## Topic 03: Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)

\### Definition

Personal Protective Equipment is equipment worn or used by a person to reduce exposure to a specific workplace Hazard.

Electrical PPE may include insulating gloves, protective footwear, eye protection, face protection, Arc-Rated clothing, helmets and hearing protection, depending on the assessed task and Risk.

\---

\### Importance

PPE provides an additional protective barrier when a Hazard cannot be completely eliminated. Correct PPE selection can reduce the severity of Shock, burn, impact and Arc-Flash injuries.

PPE is important because it:

\- Protects selected parts of the body
\- Supports safe testing and verification tasks
\- Reduces injury severity when selected correctly
\- Complements barriers, insulation and safe procedures
\- Helps meet workplace safety requirements

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Common Electrical PPE

| PPE | Main Protection | Important Limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Voltage-rated insulating gloves | Contact Shock | Must match Voltage class and inspection requirements |
| Safety glasses or goggles | Flying particles and limited eye hazards | Ordinary glasses do not provide Arc-Flash face protection |
| Arc-rated face shield or hood | Thermal energy from an Arc | Must be selected from an Arc-Flash Risk assessment |
| Arc-rated clothing | Thermal burn reduction | Ordinary synthetic clothing may melt when exposed to heat |
| Safety helmet | Impact and selected electrical hazards | Correct type and condition are essential |
| Protective footwear | Foot, impact and selected electrical protection | Does not make energized work safe by itself |
| Hearing protection | Arc-Blast noise | Does not protect against Shock or thermal energy |

\#### 2. PPE Selection

PPE must be selected according to:

\- Nature of the Hazard
\- System Voltage and available energy
\- Task and approach distance
\- Applicable standard and workplace procedure
\- Equipment rating and certification
\- User size, fit and compatibility

\#### 3. Inspection Before Use

PPE should be checked for:

\- Cracks, cuts, holes or contamination
\- Expiry or test date where applicable
\- Damaged fasteners or surfaces
\- Incorrect size or poor fit
\- Unauthorized modification

Damaged or unapproved PPE must not be used.

\#### 4. Care and Storage

PPE must be cleaned, tested, stored and replaced according to the manufacturer, applicable standards and workplace rules. Insulating equipment must be protected from sharp objects, heat, oil, chemicals and damaging sunlight.

\#### 5. PPE Limitations

PPE does not remove the Hazard. Gloves can be punctured, clothing has a limited rating and incorrect equipment can create false confidence.

The preferred sequence remains: eliminate, isolate, control and then protect against remaining Risk.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

Examples include:

\- Wearing safety glasses during wiring and drilling
\- Using inspected insulating gloves for an authorized task
\- Wearing Arc-Rated clothing where an assessment requires it
\- Using a helmet and suitable footwear in an industrial plant
\- Replacing damaged gloves before work begins
\- Storing insulating equipment in its protective container

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- PPE reduces exposure but does not eliminate an electrical Hazard.
\- PPE selection must be based on the actual Risk and system conditions.
\- Voltage rating, Arc rating, fit and compatibility are important.
\- PPE must be inspected before use.
\- Damaged or expired protective equipment must not be used.
\- Isolation and engineering controls take priority over PPE.

\## Topic 04: Common Electrical Symbols

\### Definition

An Electrical Symbol is a standardized graphical representation of an electrical component, device, connection or function used in Circuit Diagrams and technical documents.

Symbols allow complex electrical systems to be represented clearly without drawing the physical appearance of every component.

\---

\### Importance

Electrical symbols form a common technical language. They are important for:

\- Reading Schematic Diagrams
\- Drawing and documenting circuits
\- Installing and troubleshooting equipment
\- Identifying components and connections
\- Communicating between engineers and technicians
\- Answering drawing-based examination questions

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Source Symbols

Common source symbols represent:

\- Cell
\- Battery
\- DC Voltage Source
\- AC Voltage Source
\- Current Source

A Cell is commonly shown using one long and one short parallel line. A Battery uses repeated long-short line pairs.

\#### 2. Connection and Control Symbols

These include:

\- Connecting wire
\- Junction or connected crossing
\- Non-connected crossing
\- Open Switch
\- Closed Switch
\- Push Button
\- Relay Contact

A junction dot generally indicates an electrical connection. Diagram conventions must be read carefully because standards and drawing practices may differ.

\#### 3. Passive Component Symbols

Common passive components include:

\- Resistor
\- Variable Resistor
\- Capacitor
\- Polarized Capacitor
\- Inductor
\- Transformer

IEC and other drawing systems may use different graphical forms for the same component, such as rectangular or zigzag Resistor symbols.

\#### 4. Protection and Load Symbols

These include:

\- Fuse
\- Circuit Breaker
\- Lamp
\- Motor
\- Generator
\- Heater
\- Buzzer

\#### 5. Earth and Ground Symbols

Different symbols may represent Protective Earth, functional Earth or Circuit Ground. They must not be assumed to have identical purposes.

\#### 6. Instrument Symbols

A circle containing a letter is commonly used:

| Letter | Instrument |
|---|---|
| A | Ammeter |
| V | Voltmeter |
| W | Wattmeter |
| Hz | Frequency Meter |
| Ω | Ohmmeter |

\#### 7. Symbol and Physical Appearance

A symbol describes electrical function, not necessarily the shape, size, terminal layout or rating of the real component. The drawing, datasheet and equipment label must be used together.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

Electrical symbols are used in:

\- House-wiring diagrams
\- Printed Circuit Board schematics
\- Motor-control diagrams
\- Single-line power diagrams
\- Instrument and control drawings
\- Maintenance manuals
\- Equipment datasheets
\- Laboratory circuit instructions

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Electrical symbols represent components and functions graphically.
\- Symbols make circuit communication clear and compact.
\- A junction dot normally indicates connected conductors.
\- Earth and Ground symbols may represent different functions.
\- IEC and other symbol conventions can differ.
\- A symbol does not show every physical detail or rating.

\## Topic 05: Basic Electrical Tools

\### Definition

Basic Electrical Tools are hand tools and supporting devices used to prepare, connect, install, inspect and maintain electrical wiring and equipment.

Electrical tools must be suitable for the task, properly maintained and used only within their intended rating and purpose.

\---

\### Importance

Correct tools improve safety, accuracy and work quality. An unsuitable or damaged tool can injure the user, damage conductors and create weak connections that later overheat.

Basic tools are important for:

\- Cutting and stripping conductors
\- Tightening electrical terminals
\- Crimping lugs and connectors
\- Holding and bending wire
\- Checking dimensions and alignment
\- Identifying circuits and cables
\- Supporting installation and troubleshooting

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Screwdrivers

Flat, Phillips and other screwdriver types must match the screw head. The correct tip reduces slipping and terminal damage.

An insulated handle alone does not prove that a screwdriver is approved for energized electrical work. Its marking, rating and condition must be verified.

\#### 2. Pliers and Cutters

Common types include:

\- Combination Pliers
\- Long-Nose Pliers
\- Side Cutters
\- Cable Cutters

Cutters must not be used on energized conductors unless the task, tool rating and authorized procedure specifically allow it.

\#### 3. Wire Stripper

A Wire Stripper removes insulation without cutting or weakening conductor strands. The correct size slot should be selected for the conductor.

\#### 4. Crimping Tool

A Crimping Tool compresses a compatible terminal or lug onto a conductor. Tool, die, terminal and conductor sizes must match.

Pliers are not a proper substitute for a specified crimping tool.

\#### 5. Cable and Utility Tools

Other useful tools include:

\- Measuring Tape
\- Cable Knife used with suitable training
\- Spanner and Socket Set
\- Hex Key Set
\- Fish Tape or Draw Wire
\- Cable Tie Tool
\- Labeling Tool
\- Flashlight

\#### 6. Tool Inspection

Before use, check for:

\- Cracked insulation or handles
\- Loose joints and damaged jaws
\- Worn cutting edges
\- Corrosion or contamination
\- Incorrect size or rating
\- Unauthorized modification

\#### 7. Tool Discipline

Tools should be kept clean, dry, organized and protected. A tool must not be used as a hammer, lever or substitute for another tool unless designed for that purpose.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

Basic tools are used for:

\- Preparing conductors for a terminal block
\- Installing switches and sockets
\- Crimping a cable lug
\- Wiring a laboratory Circuit
\- Labeling a control panel
\- Routing cable through conduit
\- Replacing a damaged plug with power isolated

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Correct tools improve safety and connection quality.
\- Tool type, size, rating and condition must match the task.
\- A Wire Stripper protects conductor strands from damage.
\- Crimping requires compatible terminals, conductors and dies.
\- Damaged tools must be removed from service.
\- Tools must not be misused or assumed safe from appearance alone.

\## Topic 06: Measuring Instruments (Ammeter, Voltmeter, Ohmmeter, Multimeter, Clamp Meter)

\### Definition

An Electrical Measuring Instrument is a device used to determine the magnitude or condition of an electrical quantity such as Current, Voltage or Resistance.

The selected instrument must be suitable for the signal type, expected magnitude, environment and measurement category.

\---

\### Importance

Measurements connect theory with real electrical systems. Instruments help engineers and technicians verify operation, locate faults and compare actual values with calculated values.

Measuring instruments are important for:

\- Laboratory experiments
\- Installation testing
\- Equipment maintenance
\- Fault diagnosis
\- Quality control
\- Energy monitoring
\- Circuit verification

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Instrument Comparison

| Instrument | Main Quantity | Typical Connection | Important Rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ammeter | Current | Series | Never place a low-Resistance Current input directly across a Voltage source |
| Voltmeter | Voltage | Parallel | Select AC or DC and a suitable range |
| Ohmmeter | Resistance | Across isolated component or path | Circuit power must be OFF and stored energy discharged |
| Multimeter | Multiple quantities | Depends on selected function | Leads, sockets, function and range must all agree |
| Clamp Meter | Current and sometimes other quantities | Clamp around one conductor | Clamping around both outgoing and return conductors may cancel the reading |

\#### 2. Ammeter

An Ammeter measures Current and is connected in series so that branch Current passes through the instrument.

An ideal Ammeter has zero internal Resistance. A practical Ammeter has very low Resistance and a limited Current rating.

\#### 3. Voltmeter

A Voltmeter measures Potential Difference between two points and is connected in parallel.

An ideal Voltmeter has infinite internal Resistance. A practical Voltmeter has high but finite input Resistance.

\#### 4. Ohmmeter

An Ohmmeter uses its internal source to estimate Resistance. Applying an Ohmmeter to an energized circuit can damage the instrument and create a Hazard.

Parallel paths may affect an in-circuit Resistance reading.

\#### 5. Multimeter

A Digital Multimeter may measure:

\- DC and AC Voltage
\- DC and sometimes AC Current
\- Resistance
\- Continuity
\- Diode behavior
\- Capacitance, Frequency or Temperature on selected models

Changing only the rotary switch is not always sufficient. The red lead may also need to be moved between Voltage/Resistance and Current sockets.

\#### 6. Clamp Meter

A Clamp Meter measures Current by sensing the magnetic field around a conductor. Many models measure AC Current, while selected models also measure DC Current.

The jaw should normally surround one conductor. If it surrounds both supply and return conductors carrying equal opposite Currents, their magnetic fields largely cancel.

\#### 7. Range, Resolution and Accuracy

Range is the interval an instrument can measure.

Resolution is the smallest displayed change.

Accuracy indicates how close a reading is expected to be to the true value under specified conditions.

High resolution does not automatically mean high accuracy.

\#### 8. Instrument Rating

The maximum Voltage or Current printed on an instrument is not the only safety consideration. Input protection, lead rating, fuse type, measurement category and transient environment must also be suitable.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

Measuring instruments are used to:

\- Check a battery Voltage
\- Measure load Current
\- Test a fuse for continuity with power isolated
\- Measure a resistor outside the circuit
\- Check Voltage drop across a load
\- Measure motor Current with a Clamp Meter
\- Compare three-phase conductor Currents
\- Diagnose an open connection

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- An Ammeter is connected in series.
\- A Voltmeter is connected in parallel.
\- Resistance is measured only on a de-energized and safely discharged circuit.
\- A Multimeter function, range, sockets and leads must match the task.
\- A Clamp Meter normally surrounds one conductor.
\- Resolution and accuracy are not the same.
\- Instrument and lead ratings must suit the measurement environment.

\## Topic 07: Safe Measurement Practices

\### Definition

Safe Measurement Practices are the planned steps used to obtain an electrical reading while controlling Shock, Arc, short-circuit and equipment-damage risks.

Safe measurement depends on the person, instrument, leads, circuit condition, environment and procedure.

\---

\### Importance

Testing can expose a person to a Hazard because the circuit may need to be approached before its condition is known. Incorrect meter setup can also create a short circuit through the instrument.

Safe practices are important for:

\- Verifying isolation
\- Preventing incorrect Current connections
\- Protecting test leads and meters
\- Avoiding contact with exposed conductors
\- Obtaining reliable readings
\- Preventing false conclusions during troubleshooting

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Plan the Measurement

Before testing:

\- Identify the quantity to be measured.
\- Estimate the expected value.
\- Identify AC or DC.
\- Review the Circuit Diagram and equipment rating.
\- Assess electrical and environmental hazards.
\- Decide whether the measurement can be made de-energized.

\#### 2. Inspect the Instrument

Check:

\- Meter body and display
\- Test-lead insulation
\- Probe guards and tips
\- Input sockets
\- Internal fuse rating where applicable
\- Battery indication
\- Function, range and category rating

\#### 3. Correct Setup

For Voltage measurement:

1\. Place leads in the common and Voltage sockets.
2\. Select AC or DC Voltage.
3\. Select a range above the expected value when manual ranging is used.
4\. Connect in parallel across the two points.

For Current measurement using leads:

1\. De-energize before opening the Circuit where practical.
2\. Move the lead to the correct fused Current socket.
3\. Select the correct Current function and range.
4\. Connect the meter in series.
5\. Never connect this Current setup directly across a Voltage source.

\#### 4. Resistance and Continuity

Before measuring Resistance or continuity:

\- Isolate all power sources.
\- Apply required lockout and tagout.
\- Discharge stored energy using an approved method.
\- Verify absence of Voltage.
\- Consider parallel paths that may affect the reading.

\#### 5. Verify the Tester

When verifying absence of Voltage, an approved procedure commonly checks the tester on a known source before and after testing the target circuit. This helps confirm that the tester was functional during the verification.

The exact method must follow the applicable workplace procedure and instrument instructions.

\#### 6. Probe Control

Keep fingers behind probe guards, avoid exposed metal and prevent probe tips from bridging adjacent terminals.

Use suitable clips or accessories only when they are correctly rated and can be installed safely.

\#### 7. After Measurement

After testing:

\- Remove probes safely.
\- Return the red lead from the Current socket when appropriate.
\- Switch the meter to a safe storage setting.
\- Record the reading, range and condition.
\- Report abnormal results or damaged equipment.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

Safe measurement practices are applied when:

\- Verifying a circuit is de-energized
\- Measuring a battery or power-supply output
\- Checking continuity of an isolated cable
\- Measuring load Current in a laboratory
\- Checking Current with a Clamp Meter
\- Troubleshooting a control panel under an approved procedure
\- Testing household wiring by a competent person

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Every measurement requires planning and Hazard assessment.
\- Meter, leads, function, range and input sockets must match the task.
\- A Current-input mistake can create a short circuit.
\- Resistance and continuity tests require an isolated circuit.
\- Absence of Voltage must be verified, not assumed.
\- Probe control reduces accidental contact and bridging.
\- Energized testing is restricted to trained and authorized persons.

\## Topic 08: SI Units, Symbols and Common Electrical Quantities

\### Definition

The International System of Units, known as SI, is the internationally accepted system used to express physical and electrical quantities consistently.

A Quantity describes what is measured, a Quantity Symbol represents it in equations and a Unit expresses its measured magnitude.

\---

\### Importance

Correct SI notation prevents misunderstanding in calculations, drawings, measurements and technical communication.

SI Units and symbols are important for:

\- Engineering calculations
\- Instrument readings
\- Component ratings
\- Technical drawings
\- Laboratory reports
\- Standards and specifications
\- Competitive examinations

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Common Electrical Quantities

| Quantity | Common Quantity Symbol | SI Unit | Unit Symbol |
|---|---|---|---|
| Electric Current | I | ampere | A |
| Voltage or Potential Difference | V | volt | V |
| Resistance | R | ohm | Ω |
| Conductance | G | siemens | S |
| Electric Charge | Q | coulomb | C |
| Power | P | watt | W |
| Energy | E or W | joule | J |
| Capacitance | C | farad | F |
| Inductance | L | henry | H |
| Frequency | f | hertz | Hz |
| Magnetic Flux | Φ | weber | Wb |
| Magnetic Flux Density | B | tesla | T |

Quantity symbols may vary by context, but unit symbols follow standardized notation.

\#### 2. Common Prefixes

| Prefix | Symbol | Factor | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| nano | n | 10⁻⁹ | 10 nF |
| micro | µ | 10⁻⁶ | 100 µA |
| milli | m | 10⁻³ | 20 mV |
| kilo | k | 10³ | 4.7 kΩ |
| mega | M | 10⁶ | 2 MΩ |
| giga | G | 10⁹ | 1 GHz |

Uppercase and lowercase symbols are not interchangeable. For example, m means milli while M means mega.

\#### 3. Unit Writing Rules

Important practices include:

\- Leave a space between a numerical value and most unit symbols: 230 V, 5 A and 10 kΩ.
\- Do not add a plural letter to a unit symbol: 5 A, not 5 As.
\- Do not normally place a period after a unit symbol unless it ends a sentence.
\- Preserve correct uppercase and lowercase letters.
\- Use one consistent unit system in a calculation.

\#### 4. Basic Relationships

\*\*I = Q / t\*\*

\*\*V = W / Q\*\*

\*\*R = V / I\*\*

\*\*P = VI\*\*

These equations connect quantities, but their units must remain consistent.

\#### 5. Prefix Conversion

Examples:

\*\*250 mA = 0.250 A\*\*

\*\*4.7 kΩ = 4700 Ω\*\*

\*\*2 µF = 2 × 10⁻⁶ F\*\*

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

SI Units are used when:

\- Reading a Multimeter display
\- Selecting a resistor or capacitor
\- Reading a motor nameplate
\- Calculating Power and Energy
\- Recording laboratory measurements
\- Comparing instrument ranges
\- Preparing technical specifications

\### Key Takeaways

\- SI provides a consistent international measurement system.
\- Quantity symbols and unit symbols are not the same thing.
\- The ampere is the SI Unit of Electric Current.

\## Topic 09: Practical Safety Rules in Home, Laboratory and Industry

\### Definition

Practical Electrical Safety Rules are situation-specific actions used to prevent injury and equipment damage in homes, laboratories and industrial workplaces.

The level of Hazard differs between locations, but the basic principles of isolation, correct ratings, inspection, training and verification remain applicable.

\---

\### Importance

Rules must be converted into daily behavior. Familiar surroundings can create false confidence, and repeated unsafe shortcuts may eventually cause a serious incident.

Practical rules help:

\- Prevent common household accidents
\- Protect students and laboratory equipment
\- Control industrial energy sources
\- Improve emergency response
\- Maintain safe and reliable installations
\- Build professional habits

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Home Safety Rules

\- Do not touch switches, plugs or appliances with wet hands.
\- Keep children away from accessible electrical hazards.
\- Do not overload sockets or extension boards.
\- Replace damaged plugs, cables and accessories.
\- Use correctly rated protective devices.
\- Do not insert bare wires into a socket.
\- Isolate the supply before permitted maintenance.
\- Use competent personnel for fixed-wiring work.
\- Keep heaters and high-power appliances away from combustible materials.

\#### 2. Laboratory Safety Rules

\- Read the Circuit Diagram before wiring.
\- Keep the supply OFF while building or changing a Circuit.
\- Ask for instructor approval before energizing.
\- Use current-limited and appropriately rated supplies where possible.
\- Check component polarity and ratings.
\- Remove watches, rings and other conductive jewelry when required.
\- Keep the bench dry and organized.
\- Do not work alone on hazardous setups.
\- Know the emergency isolation point.
\- Report damaged equipment immediately.

\#### 3. Industrial Safety Rules

\- Follow the approved permit and energy-control procedure.
\- Identify every electrical and non-electrical energy source.
\- Apply Lockout and Tagout where required.
\- Verify isolation using suitable test equipment.
\- Observe approach boundaries and barriers.
\- Use task-specific PPE and rated tools.
\- Maintain safe clearance from overhead lines.
\- Keep panels closed, labeled and accessible.
\- Do not defeat an interlock or protective device.
\- Maintain communication with the responsible team.

\#### 4. Emergency Response

If a person is in contact with electricity:

1\. Do not touch the person while the electrical source may still be energized.
2\. Isolate the supply using a safe method if possible.
3\. Call local emergency services.
4\. Provide first aid or CPR only if trained and the scene is safe.
5\. Arrange medical evaluation because electrical injuries may not be externally visible.

For an electrical fire, isolate power when safe and use an extinguisher suitable for the energized electrical Hazard. Water must not be applied to energized electrical equipment.

\#### 5. Safety Before Speed

Production, examination deadlines and troubleshooting pressure do not justify bypassing safety controls. Stop work when conditions are unclear or different from the approved plan.

\#### 6. Local Rules and Standards

Actual electrical work must follow applicable national regulations, workplace procedures, equipment instructions and relevant standards. A textbook cannot replace task-specific training, authorization or supervision.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

Practical safety rules apply to:

\- Using household appliances
\- Building student laboratory Circuits
\- Servicing motor-control equipment
\- Working with batteries and UPS systems
\- Inspecting solar installations
\- Maintaining industrial panels
\- Responding to damaged cables or electrical fires
\- Planning safe technical work

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Home, laboratory and industrial environments require different levels of control.
\- Power should remain OFF while a laboratory Circuit is being changed.
\- Industrial maintenance requires authorized isolation and verification.
\- Never touch a Shock victim until the electrical source is controlled.
\- Water must not be used on energized electrical equipment.
\- Stop work when conditions are unsafe or unclear.
\- Local regulations, standards and procedures must always be followed.

\# Chapter Summary

\- Electrical Safety prevents Shock, burns, Arc Flash, fire and equipment damage.
\- Electrical equipment should normally be de-energized before work begins.

\- Electrical symbols provide a common language for Circuit Diagrams.
\- Correctly selected and maintained tools improve safety and work quality.
\- An Ammeter is connected in series, while a Voltmeter is connected in parallel.
\- Resistance and continuity are measured only on an isolated and safely discharged circuit.
\- A Clamp Meter normally surrounds one conductor.
\- Meter function, range, sockets, leads and rating must match the measurement.
\- SI Units and prefixes provide consistent engineering communication.
\- Home, laboratory and industrial work must follow appropriate safety rules.
\- Actual electrical work requires applicable training, authorization, standards and procedures.
