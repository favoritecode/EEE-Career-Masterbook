\# Chapter 25

\# Electrical Estimation, Load Calculation and Energy Management

\---

\## Introduction

Electrical installations must be technically safe, economically justified and capable of serving actual operating demand. Electrical Estimation converts drawings and load data into equipment ratings, material quantities, labour and cost. Load Calculation establishes Connected Load, Maximum Demand, demand, diversity and utilization. Energy Management then measures how efficiently the installed system is used and identifies sustainable improvements.

This chapter connects these activities through definitions, formulas, worksheets and simple residential, commercial and industrial examples. Numerical percentages and tariffs used in examples are instructional unless explicitly linked to a current adopted rule. Final projects must use current local codes, utility data, manufacturer information, measured operating records and approved assumptions.

\- Apply estimation concepts to practical industrial projects.

\## Topic 01: Introduction to Electrical Estimation

\### Definition

Electrical Estimation is the systematic process of determining electrical loads, equipment ratings, material quantities, labour requirements, cost and documentation for an installation.

\---

\### Importance

An estimate converts design intent into a safe, buildable and budgeted project. Missing load or quantity data can cause overload, delay, cost growth and unsafe substitution.

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Estimation Inputs

Architectural drawings, equipment schedules, supply characteristics, occupancy, operating hours, environmental conditions, future growth and applicable standards form the input.

\#### 2. Main Outputs

\- Load schedule and Maximum Demand
\- Single-line diagram
\- Cable and protective-device schedule
\- Distribution Board schedule
\- Material take-off and bill of quantities
\- Cost, labour and project assumptions

\#### 3. Design vs Estimate

Design proves technical suitability. Estimation quantifies equipment, materials and cost. The two activities exchange information and must remain consistent.

\#### 4. Estimation Boundary

State whether the estimate includes utility service, Transformer, Generator, Earthing, lighting, controls, fire stopping, testing, civil work, taxes and contingency.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

A small office estimate may include the incoming DB, lighting and Socket circuits, Air-Conditioner Feeders, Cable containment, Earthing, testing and labeled as-built drawings.

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Estimation needs verified drawings and load data.
\- Technical design and cost estimation are connected.
\- The scope boundary must be written.
\- Schedules and quantities must agree.
\- Assumptions require approval.

\## Topic 02: Objectives and Importance of Electrical Estimation

\### Definition

The objectives of Electrical Estimation are to establish safe capacities, accurate quantities, realistic cost and time, and a clear basis for procurement and construction.

\---

\### Importance

A complete estimate supports budgeting, tender comparison, resource planning, code compliance, change control and lifecycle decision-making.

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Safety Objective

Confirm that supply, Cables, Switchgear and protection suit calculated load, fault level, Voltage drop and environment.

\#### 2. Economic Objective

Avoid both undersizing and unjustified oversizing. Compare initial cost with Energy loss, maintenance, service life and expansion needs.

\#### 3. Procurement Objective

Specifications and quantities must identify ratings, standards, accessories and tests—not only device names.

\#### 4. Planning Objective

Labour, delivery, shutdown, access, temporary supply and commissioning requirements influence schedule and cost.

\#### 5. Change Control

A revised load or route must update calculations, drawings, quantities and price together.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

Replacing a planned 30 kW Motor with a 55 kW unit changes Feeder Current, starting study, Cable, starter, protection, containment and possibly Transformer demand.

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Estimation supports safety and economics.
\- Lifecycle cost can justify efficient equipment.
\- Procurement descriptions need technical ratings.
\- Time and access are part of cost.
\- Every design change must propagate through the estimate.

\## Topic 03: Types of Electrical Loads

\### Definition

Electrical loads are classified by purpose and electrical behavior, including lighting, heating, Motor, electronic, continuous, intermittent, critical and nonlinear loads.

\---

\### Importance

Load type affects Current, Power Factor, starting duty, harmonics, diversity, protection, backup supply and Energy use.

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Load Categories

| Load Type | Typical Behavior | Design Concern |
|---|---|---|
| Resistive heating | PF near unity | Sustained thermal load |
| Motor | Starting Current and Reactive Power | Voltage dip and overload |
| LED/electronic | Nonlinear input | Harmonics and inrush |
| Welding/lift | Intermittent and fluctuating | Demand and flicker |
| Critical load | Must remain available | Redundancy and backup |
| Regenerative load | May return Power | Bidirectional protection |

\#### 2. Continuous and Intermittent Loads

Operating duration influences conductor and protective-device sizing under the applicable code. Intermittent duty still requires thermal and demand assessment.

\#### 3. Linear and Nonlinear Loads

Nonlinear loads draw distorted Current. RMS Current, Neutral harmonics and Power Factor must be considered.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

A commercial building may contain LED lighting, lifts, VFD pumps, UPS systems, computers and resistive water heaters, each with different demand and harmonic behavior.

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Classify loads before calculating demand.
\- Motors need starting and overload checks.
\- Electronic loads may create harmonics.
\- Critical loads need continuity planning.
\- Operating duty affects estimation.

\---

\## Topic 04: Connected Load

\### Definition

Connected Load is the sum of the rated input Power or apparent Power of all equipment connected within a defined installation or circuit boundary.

\---

\### Importance

Connected Load provides an upper inventory value and supports service planning, but it is usually not equal to simultaneous Maximum Demand.

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Basic Calculation

\[ P_{connected}=\sum_{i=1}^{n} n_i P_i \]

Use input rating, not output rating, and avoid mixing kW and kVA without conversion.

\#### 2. Example

A home has 12 lamps at 12 W, 6 fans at 75 W, one 1.5 kW heater and one 1.2 kW refrigerator input rating:

\[ P_c=12(12)+6(75)+1500+1200=3294\text{ W}=3.294\text{ kW} \]

\#### 3. Three-Phase and Electronic Loads

Record phase, Voltage, PF, efficiency and harmonic data where relevant. Manufacturer nameplate or verified input data should be used.

\#### 4. Load Schedule

A useful schedule includes tag, description, quantity, rating, phase, PF, efficiency, demand allowance, circuit and operating status.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

An estimator totals every final load by DB and then reconciles each DB total with the upstream Main Switchboard schedule.

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Connected Load is the sum of installed load ratings.
\- Use electrical input ratings.
\- Define the estimation boundary.
\- Do not mix kW and kVA directly.
\- Reconcile downstream and upstream schedules.

\---

\### Reference

\- \*\*Primary Textbook\*\*: Electrical Wiring: Residential
\- \*\*Authors\*\*: Ray C. Mullin and Phil Simmons
\- \*\*Relevant Chapter\*\*: Connected loads and dwelling load schedules
\- \*\*Supporting Text\*\*: Electrical Installation Design Guide — Institution of Engineering and Technology — Load assessment and circuit schedules

\## Topic 05: Maximum Demand

\### Definition

Maximum Demand is the highest average load measured or expected over a specified demand interval during a stated period.

\---

\### Importance

Maximum Demand determines practical supply and equipment loading more realistically than simply adding all nameplates.

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Demand Interval

A 15-minute, 30-minute or other interval may be used by the utility or study. Instantaneous peak and interval Maximum Demand are not necessarily equal.

\#### 2. Estimated Maximum Demand

\[ P_{MD}=P_{connected}\times\text{Demand Factor} \]

Apply demand allowances by load group using the governing code or verified operating data; do not use one arbitrary percentage for every load.

\#### 3. Example

If Connected Load is 120 kW and justified Demand Factor is 0.65:

\[ P_{MD}=120\times0.65=78\text{ kW} \]

At 400 V, Three-Phase, PF 0.90:

\[ I=\frac{78,000}{\sqrt3\times400\times0.90}=125.1\text{ A} \]

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

A commercial building's recorded 30-minute peak may occur during afternoon cooling operation even though its total connected load is much higher.

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Maximum Demand needs a stated interval and period.
\- It is normally below Connected Load.
\- Use justified demand data.
\- Convert kW to Current using Voltage and PF.
\- Future growth may be considered separately.

\---

\### Reference

\- \*\*Primary Textbook\*\*: Electrical Installation Design Guide
\- \*\*Authors\*\*: The Institution of Engineering and Technology
\- \*\*Relevant Chapter\*\*: Maximum Demand and load assessment
\- \*\*Supporting Text\*\*: IEEE Std 3001.1 — Planning and design of industrial and commercial Power systems

\## Topic 06: Demand Factor

\### Definition

Demand Factor is the ratio of Maximum Demand to Connected Load for the same system boundary and period.

\---

\### Importance

It converts installed load inventory into an estimated simultaneous demand and helps size services and distribution equipment economically.

\---

\### Main Concepts

\[ \text{Demand Factor}=\frac{\text{Maximum Demand}}{\text{Connected Load}} \]

For the same boundary, Demand Factor is normally less than or equal to 1.

\#### Example

Connected Load = 200 kW and Maximum Demand = 130 kW:

\[ DF=\frac{130}{200}=0.65 \]

Demand Factor varies with occupancy, process, climate, time and load type. A value from one building should not be copied blindly to another.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

A hotel and a warehouse with identical Connected Load can have different Demand Factors because their operating patterns differ.

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Demand Factor = MD / Connected Load.
\- It is normally ≤ 1.
\- Use the same boundary and units.
\- Demand Factor is application-specific.
\- Document the source of every allowance.

\---

\### Reference

\- \*\*Primary Textbook\*\*: Electrical Power Distribution Handbook
\- \*\*Author\*\*: Thomas Allen Short
\- \*\*Relevant Chapter\*\*: Load estimation, demand and Distribution planning
\- \*\*Supporting Text\*\*: IEEE Std 3001.1 — Load characteristics and demand assessment

\## Topic 07: Diversity Factor

\### Definition

Diversity Factor is the ratio of the sum of individual noncoincident Maximum Demands to the Maximum Demand of the combined group.

\---

\### Importance

Diversity recognizes that different consumers or load groups reach their peaks at different times, allowing economical shared-system sizing.

\---

\### Main Concepts

\[ \text{Diversity Factor}=\frac{\sum MD_{individual}}{MD_{group}} \]

Diversity Factor is normally equal to or greater than 1.

\#### Example

Three departments have individual Maximum Demands of 30, 40 and 50 kW, while their coincident group peak is 90 kW:

\[ \text{Diversity Factor}=\frac{30+40+50}{90}=1.33 \]

Diversity Factor is not Demand Factor. The numerator and denominator must be clearly defined.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

A utility Transformer can supply many homes because their cooking, cooling and appliance peaks do not all occur at exactly the same time.

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Diversity Factor compares individual and coincident peaks.
\- It is normally ≥ 1.
\- Demand Factor and Diversity Factor are different.
\- Time coincidence creates diversity.
\- Use measured or approved data where possible.

\---

\### Reference

\- \*\*Primary Textbook\*\*: Electric Power Distribution Engineering
\- \*\*Author\*\*: Turan Gönen
\- \*\*Relevant Chapter\*\*: Load diversity and Distribution-system planning
\- \*\*Supporting Text\*\*: Electric Power Distribution Handbook — Thomas Allen Short — Coincident and diversified demand

\## Topic 08: Load Factor

\### Definition

Load Factor is the ratio of Average Load to Maximum Demand over the same stated period.

\---

\### Importance

A higher Load Factor indicates more uniform asset utilization and usually reduces cost per unit of Energy because fixed capacity serves more Energy.

\---

\### Main Concepts

\[ \text{Load Factor}=\frac{P_{average}}{P_{maximum}}=\frac{E}{P_{maximum}T} \]

It is between 0 and 1 for correctly matched data.

\#### Example

A factory uses 36,000 kWh in a 30-day month and has Maximum Demand of 100 kW:

\[ P_{avg}=\frac{36,000}{30\times24}=50\text{ kW} \]

\[ LF=\frac{50}{100}=0.50=50\% \]

Reducing short peaks without reducing useful production can improve Load Factor.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

Moving noncritical processes away from the peak period can reduce Maximum Demand while maintaining monthly production Energy.

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Load Factor = Average Load / Maximum Demand.
\- Energy, peak and time period must match.
\- A flatter load profile raises Load Factor.
\- It differs from Demand Factor.
\- Load management can improve asset utilization.

\---

\### Reference

\- \*\*Primary Textbook\*\*: Electric Power Distribution Engineering
\- \*\*Author\*\*: Turan Gönen
\- \*\*Relevant Chapter\*\*: Load Factor, load characteristics and utilization
\- \*\*Supporting Text\*\*: Electric Power Distribution Engineering — Turan Gönen — Load characteristics and system economics

\## Topic 09: Power Factor — Review

\### Definition

Power Factor is the ratio of active Power to apparent Power and indicates how effectively RMS Voltage and Current produce useful active Power.

\---

\### Importance

For the same kW and Voltage, lower Power Factor increases Current, Cable loss, Voltage drop and kVA demand.

\---

\### Main Concepts

\[ PF=\frac{P}{S} \]

For balanced Three-Phase loads:

\[ P=\sqrt3 V_L I_L PF \]

\#### Example

A 90 kW load at 400 V draws:

At PF 0.75: \(I=173.2\text{ A}\)

At PF 0.95: \(I=136.7\text{ A}\)

Improvement reduces upstream Current but does not reduce the load's useful kW. Capacitor Banks require harmonic, resonance, switching and overcompensation checks.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

An industrial customer may reduce kVA demand and I²R losses by installing automatically controlled Power-Factor correction near inductive loads.

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- PF = kW / kVA.
\- Low PF increases Current for the same kW.
\- PF correction releases network capacity.
\- It does not directly reduce useful kWh.
\- Harmonics and resonance must be checked.

\---

\### Reference

\- \*\*Primary Textbook\*\*: Electrical Machines, Drives and Power Systems
\- \*\*Author\*\*: Theodore Wildi
\- \*\*Relevant Chapter\*\*: Power Factor and Reactive Power fundamentals
\- \*\*Supporting Text\*\*: IEEE Std 3002.8 — Harmonic studies; industrial PF-correction application considerations

\## Topic 10: Electrical Load Calculation for Residential Buildings

\### Definition

Residential load calculation determines Connected Load and justified Maximum Demand for dwelling lighting, Socket-Outlets and fixed appliances.

\---

\### Importance

Correct calculation supports safe service, DB, Cable and protection selection without assuming every appliance operates simultaneously.

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Simple Worksheet

| Load | Quantity × Rating | Connected Load | Demand Allowance | Estimated Demand |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Lighting | 10 × 12 W | 0.12 kW | 100% | 0.12 kW |
| Fans | 5 × 75 W | 0.375 kW | 80% | 0.30 kW |
| General Sockets | Assessed | 2.0 kW | 40% | 0.80 kW |
| Refrigerator | 1 × 0.4 kW | 0.40 kW | 100% | 0.40 kW |
| Air Conditioner | 2 × 1.5 kW | 3.0 kW | 75% | 2.25 kW |
| Water Heater | 1 × 2 kW | 2.0 kW | 100% | 2.0 kW |

Estimated MD = 5.87 kW. The example percentages are instructional only; use locally approved rules.

At 230 V, PF 0.95:

\[ I\approx\frac{5870}{230\times0.95}=26.9\text{ A} \]

Motor starting, future load, phase allocation and circuit-specific rules require separate checks.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

A multi-apartment building calculates each dwelling, common lighting, lift and pump loads separately before applying justified coincidence at the main service.

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- List every fixed and general load.
\- Apply demand by load group.
\- Instructional percentages are not code values.
\- Check PF and starting Current.
\- Balance Single-Phase loads across phases.

\---

\### Reference

\- \*\*Primary Textbook\*\*: Electrical Wiring: Residential
\- \*\*Authors\*\*: Ray C. Mullin and Phil Simmons
\- \*\*Relevant Chapter\*\*: Dwelling load calculations and service sizing
\- \*\*Supporting Text\*\*: IEC 60364 series and locally adopted wiring rules — Load assessment and installation design

\## Topic 11: Electrical Load Calculation for Commercial Buildings

\### Definition

Commercial load calculation assesses lighting, Socket, HVAC, lifts, pumps, IT, kitchen, signage, emergency and tenant loads using appropriate operating and coincidence data.

\---

\### Importance

Commercial buildings have time-varying occupancy, substantial cooling and electronic loads, so demand, harmonics and backup classification are important.

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Example Worksheet

| Load Group | Connected kW | Demand Factor | Estimated Demand |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Lighting | 30 | 0.90 | 27 kW |
| Sockets/IT | 40 | 0.60 | 24 kW |
| HVAC | 90 | 0.80 | 72 kW |
| Lifts | 30 | 0.50 | 15 kW |
| Pumps | 20 | 0.70 | 14 kW |

Total Connected Load = 210 kW; estimated MD = 152 kW.

At 400 V Three-Phase and PF 0.90:

\[ I=\frac{152,000}{\sqrt3\times400\times0.90}=243.8\text{ A} \]

This is a preliminary demand estimate. Diversity between groups, Motor starting, harmonics, redundancy and code rules need engineering verification.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

A shopping center may peak during simultaneous cooling and customer activity, while its fire pumps remain emergency loads and are not treated like normal diversified loads.

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Separate load groups by behavior.
\- HVAC often dominates demand.
\- Critical and fire loads need special treatment.
\- Check harmonics and Neutral loading.
\- Validate preliminary demand with code or operating data.

\---

\- \*\*Authors\*\*: Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers

\## Topic 12: Cable Size Selection Based on Load

\### Definition

Load-based Cable selection determines a preliminary conductor size from Design Current and then verifies installation derating, Voltage drop, fault withstand and protective coordination.

\---

\### Importance

A Cable sized only from nominal load Current may overheat or fail other requirements under actual installation conditions.

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Selection Sequence

Calculate \(I_b\) → select protective rating \(I_n\) → find corrected Cable capacity \(I_z\) → check Voltage drop → check fault withstand → verify terminals and environment.

A simplified coordination condition is:

\[ I_b\leq I_n\leq I_z \]

Use exact code requirements for overload and fault protection.

\#### 2. Example

A 30 kW, 400 V Three-Phase load at PF 0.85 and efficiency 0.92:

\[ I_b=\frac{30,000}{\sqrt3\times400\times0.85\times0.92}=55.4\text{ A} \]

The final Cable cannot be chosen from 55.4 A alone; grouping, ambient temperature, route, starting, Voltage drop and fault duration must be checked.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

A grouped Cable in a hot ceiling may require a larger area than the same Cable installed alone on an open tray.

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Calculate Design Current correctly.
\- Apply all correction factors.
\- Check Voltage drop and fault duty.
\- Coordinate Cable and protective device.
\- Use current tables and manufacturer data.

\---

\### Reference

\- \*\*Primary Textbook\*\*: Electrical Installation Design Guide
\- \*\*Authors\*\*: The Institution of Engineering and Technology
\- \*\*Relevant Chapter\*\*: Cable sizing, correction factors, Voltage drop and protection

\## Topic 13: Selection of Protective Devices Based on Load

\### Definition

Protective-device selection matches device type, Current rating, trip characteristic, poles, breaking capacity and residual-current function to the load, Cable and fault duty.

\---

\### Importance

A device must carry normal and starting Current without unwanted operation while clearing overload, Short Circuit or Earth Fault within required limits.

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Basic Checks

\- Design Current and Cable capacity
\- Motor starting or transformer inrush
\- Prospective fault Current
\- Breaking and making capacity
\- Earthing arrangement and disconnection time
\- Selectivity with upstream/downstream devices
\- Ambient and enclosure derating

\#### 2. Device Application

MCBs suit many final circuits; MCCBs suit larger adjustable duties; RCCBs provide residual-current protection without normal integral Overcurrent protection; RCBOs combine both.

\#### 3. Example

For a 55.4 A Design Current, a nominal device may be selected above load Current only after verifying the Cable corrected capacity, trip curve, starting Current and standard rating. A 63 A device is not automatically correct.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

A Motor Feeder may use Short-Circuit protection plus a separate overload Relay coordinated with the Motor starting curve.

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Device rating must exceed normal Design Current appropriately.
\- Breaking capacity must exceed fault duty.
\- Trip curve must tolerate legitimate inrush.
\- RCCB alone is not overload protection.
\- Verify selectivity and disconnection time.

\---

\### Reference

\- \*\*Primary Textbook\*\*: Electrical Installation Design Guide
\- \*\*Authors\*\*: The Institution of Engineering and Technology
\- \*\*Relevant Chapter\*\*: Protective-device selection and coordination
\- \*\*Supporting Text\*\*: IEC 60898-1, IEC 60947-2 and IEC 61008/61009 series — LV protective-device requirements

\## Topic 14: Electrical Energy Consumption Calculation

\### Definition

Electrical Energy consumption is active Power integrated over operating time, commonly measured in kilowatt-hour.

\---

\### Importance

Energy calculation supports billing, equipment comparison, Energy auditing and savings verification.

\---

\### Main Concepts

For constant Power:

\[ E=P\times t \]

For several loads:

\[ E_{total}=\sum P_i t_i \]

\#### Example

Ten 12 W lamps operate 6 hours/day for 30 days:

\[ E=10\times0.012\times6\times30=21.6\text{ kWh} \]

A 1.5 kW Air Conditioner does not necessarily draw nameplate Power continuously; thermostat cycling and variable speed affect actual Energy. Metered data is preferred for accurate assessment.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

A facility submeter can record each department's kWh so operating changes can be compared with production or floor area.

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Energy = Power × time for constant load.
\- Convert W to kW before calculating kWh.
\- Use actual input Power and operating hours.
\- Cycling loads need measured or duty-cycle data.
\- Match the measurement boundary and period.

\---

\### Reference

\- \*\*Primary Textbook\*\*: Energy Management Handbook
\- \*\*Authors\*\*: Wayne C. Turner and Steve Doty
\- \*\*Relevant Chapter\*\*: Energy measurement, load profiles and performance indicators
\- \*\*Supporting Text\*\*: ISO 50001 — Energy-management systems

\## Topic 15: Electricity Billing — Basic Concept

\### Definition

Electricity billing converts metered Energy, demand and other tariff components into a customer charge under the applicable utility schedule.

\---

\### Importance

Understanding the bill helps identify Energy, Maximum-Demand, Power-Factor and time-of-use cost opportunities.

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Possible Bill Components

\- Energy charge: kWh × tariff
\- Demand charge: billed kW or kVA × rate
\- Fixed or customer charge
\- Power-Factor adjustment
\- Time-of-use charge
\- Taxes, duties and regulatory adjustments

\#### 2. Instructional Example

If Energy = 500 kWh, illustrative Energy rate = 10 currency units/kWh and fixed charge = 300:

\[ Bill=500\times10+300=5300 \]

This is not a current Bangladesh tariff. Actual tariff blocks, demand, VAT, minimum charge and adjustments must be taken from the current utility publication.

\#### 3. Bill Verification

Check meter multiplier, billing days, previous/current readings, tariff category and demand interval.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

A commercial customer may reduce cost by controlling a short demand peak even if total monthly kWh remains nearly unchanged.

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Bills may include Energy and demand.
\- Tariffs change and must be verified.
\- PF and time-of-use may affect cost.
\- Check meter multipliers and billing period.
\- Do not present an illustrative rate as an official tariff.

\---

\## Topic 16: Energy Management

\### Definition

Energy Management is the organized process of measuring, analyzing and controlling Energy use to improve performance without compromising safety, quality or required service.

\---

\### Importance

A structured system converts isolated savings ideas into sustained technical and financial improvement.

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Energy-Management Cycle

\[ \text{Policy}\rightarrow\text{Measure}\rightarrow\text{Analyze}\rightarrow\text{Plan}\rightarrow\text{Implement}\rightarrow\text{Verify}\rightarrow\text{Improve} \]

\#### 2. Baseline and EnPI

An Energy baseline represents reference consumption. An Energy Performance Indicator may be kWh per unit product, kWh/m² or kWh per occupied room, adjusted for relevant variables.

\#### 3. Significant Energy Uses

Identify systems with high consumption or improvement potential, such as HVAC, compressed air, Motors, boilers, lighting and process heating.

\#### 4. Measurement and Verification

Savings are the difference between adjusted baseline and reporting-period use—not simply the reduction between two unrelated bills.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

A textile plant tracks kWh per kilogram of fabric, correcting for production volume before judging whether a Motor-drive project saved Energy.

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Management begins with measurement.
\- Use baselines and meaningful EnPIs.
\- Prioritize significant Energy uses.
\- Verify savings against relevant variables.
\- Continual improvement preserves results.

\---

\### Reference

\- \*\*Authors\*\*: Wayne C. Turner and Steve Doty

\## Topic 17: Energy Conservation Techniques

\### Definition

Energy conservation techniques reduce avoidable Energy use through operational control, maintenance, efficient design and technology improvement.

\---

\### Importance

Many low-cost measures save Energy immediately, while capital projects require technical and financial evaluation.

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Home

Use efficient lighting, appropriate thermostat settings, appliance shutdown, good building envelope and right-sized equipment.

\#### 2. Commercial Buildings

Apply occupancy/daylight control, HVAC scheduling, set-point management, clean filters, Building Management Systems and demand control.

\#### 3. Industry

Repair compressed-air leaks, optimize pressure, maintain steam systems, use efficient Motors and VFDs where variable flow exists, improve PF and schedule loads.

\#### 4. Avoid False Savings

Reducing ventilation below health requirements, bypassing protection or lowering lighting below task needs is not acceptable conservation.

\#### 5. Financial Evaluation

Simple Payback = Investment / Annual Saving. Lifecycle analysis should also include maintenance, replacement, discount rate and risk.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

Reducing fan speed with a VFD can save substantial Power in a variable-flow system, but throttling, minimum flow and harmonic effects must be assessed.

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Start with no-cost operational measures.
\- Maintenance restores efficiency.
\- Match technology to load profile.
\- Never sacrifice safety or service.
\- Verify savings after implementation.

\## Topic 18: Energy-Efficient Electrical Equipment

\### Definition

Energy-efficient equipment delivers required service with lower lifecycle Energy, loss or demand under relevant operating conditions.

\---

\### Importance

\#### 1. Efficient Equipment Examples

\- High-efficacy LED luminaires
\- High-efficiency Motors
\- Low-loss Transformers
\- Inverter Air Conditioners
\- Variable Frequency Drives
\- Efficient UPS and Power supplies
\- Automatic control and metering

\#### 2. Part-Load Performance

Equipment frequently operates below rated load. Compare efficiency curves and standby loss, not only full-load efficiency.

\### Real-Life Applications

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Right-size equipment.

\- Compatibility and reliability remain essential.

\## Topic 19: Electrical Estimation Procedure

\### Definition

Electrical Estimation Procedure is the controlled sequence used to develop load, design, quantity, cost and tender information from an approved scope.

\---

\### Importance

A repeatable procedure improves accuracy, reviewability and change control.

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Procedure

1. Define scope, exclusions and standards.
2. Review drawings and survey the site.
3. Prepare load and operating schedules.
4. Calculate demand, PF and supply capacity.
5. Prepare single-line and circuit architecture.
6. Size Cables, protection, DBs and containment.
7. Check Voltage drop, fault duty, Earthing and selectivity.
8. Take off material quantities.
9. Add labour, equipment, testing and indirect cost.
10. Review, reconcile and state assumptions.

\#### 2. Material Take-Off

Measure route lengths from scaled or digital drawings, then account for vertical drops, termination allowance and approved wastage. Do not hide arbitrary allowances.

\#### 3. Review

Cross-check DB schedules, Cable schedules, drawings and BOQ. Independent review is valuable for large projects.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

A tender estimate is issued with a scope sheet, assumptions, BOQ, technical specifications, load summary and revision number.

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Define scope before quantity take-off.
\- Calculate loads before sizing equipment.
\- Reconcile all schedules.
\- State controlled allowances.
\- Use revision and independent review.

\---

\### Reference

\- \*\*Primary Textbook\*\*: Electrical Estimating Methods
\- \*\*Author\*\*: Wayne J. Del Pico
\- \*\*Relevant Chapter\*\*: Quantity take-off, labour, pricing and estimate review

\## Topic 20: Practical Estimation Examples

\### Definition

A practical estimation example combines load inventory, demand, Current, preliminary equipment rating, Cable checks, quantities and cost assumptions for a defined project.

\---

\### Importance

Worked examples help learners connect formulas with the decision sequence while recognizing that final design needs current code data.

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### Example: Small Workshop

Connected loads:

\- Lighting: 2 kW
\- Sockets: 5 kW
\- Motor A: 7.5 kW input
\- Motor B: 11 kW input
\- Ventilation: 3 kW

Connected Load = 28.5 kW.

Assume only for this example: lighting 100%, Sockets 50%, both Motors 100%, ventilation 80%.

\[ MD=2+2.5+7.5+11+2.4=25.4\text{ kW} \]

At 400 V, Three-Phase, PF 0.85:

\[ I=\frac{25,400}{\sqrt3\times400\times0.85}=43.1\text{ A} \]

Next checks: Motor starting, Cable derating, Voltage drop, fault level, protection, phase balance, future load and local demand rules.

\#### Worksheet Columns

Item | Quantity | Input Rating | Connected kW/kVA | Demand basis | MD | Phase | PF | Hours | Circuit | Remarks.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

An estimator uses the worksheet to prepare a preliminary Main DB rating, then completes fault and Cable studies before procurement.

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Examples require stated assumptions.
\- Use input Power and common units.
\- Preliminary Current is not final Cable size.
\- Motor starting needs separate review.
\- Worksheet traceability supports checking.

\## Topic 21: Common Mistakes in Load Calculation

\### Definition

Common load-calculation mistakes are errors in data, units, boundaries, demand assumptions, coincidence, PF, efficiency or duplicated equipment that produce an incorrect supply estimate.

\---

\### Importance

A mathematically correct formula can still give a dangerous result when its inputs or assumptions are wrong.

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### Frequent Mistakes

| Mistake | Consequence | Prevention |
|---|---|---|
| Using output kW as input kW | Current underestimated | Include efficiency |
| Adding kW and kVA directly | Invalid total | Convert using PF |
| Confusing demand and diversity | Wrong MD | Write each formula |
| Applying diversity twice | Undersized supply | Track hierarchy |
| Ignoring Motor starting | Voltage dip/trip | Check starting duty |
| Ignoring harmonics | Neutral/thermal overload | Assess nonlinear loads |
| Copying old tariff | Wrong cost | Verify current utility data |
| Duplicating standby load | Oversized estimate | Define operating scenario |

Also avoid mixing phase and line Voltage, using inconsistent time periods and forgetting future approved loads.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

If a Motor is rated 22 kW mechanical output, using 22 kW as electrical input underestimates Current because efficiency is below 100%.

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Verify nameplate input/output meaning.
\- Keep kW, kVA and kvar separate.
\- Apply diversity once at the correct level.
\- Check starting and harmonics.
\- Audit formulas, units and assumptions.

\---

\### Reference

\- \*\*Primary Textbook\*\*: Standard Handbook for Electrical Engineers
\- \*\*Authors\*\*: Surya Santoso and H. Wayne Beaty, editors
\- \*\*Relevant Chapter\*\*: Load, units and system-design checking
\- \*\*Supporting Text\*\*: IEEE Std 3001.1 — Load data and planning practices

\## Topic 22: Safety Considerations in Electrical Installation Design

\### Definition

Safety considerations are design measures that prevent Electric Shock, fire, Arc Flash, thermal damage and unsafe operation throughout installation life.

\---

\### Importance

A low-cost estimate is unacceptable if it omits protective conductors, adequate breaking capacity, safe access, testing or required emergency systems.

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Design Safety Checks

\- Earthing and automatic disconnection
\- Cable thermal and fault withstand
\- Protective-device breaking capacity
\- Residual-current protection where required
\- Selectivity and Arc-Flash assessment
\- Isolation, access and labels
\- Fire stopping and Cable fire performance
\- Environmental/IP and hazardous-area suitability

\#### 2. Safe Maintainability

Provide working space, isolation points, interlocks, test access and unambiguous circuit identification.

\#### 3. Emergency and Critical Loads

Life-safety systems require code-compliant circuit integrity, source arrangement and separation. Normal diversity rules may not apply in the same way.

\#### 4. Competence and Verification

Design, installation, inspection and testing require competent persons and controlled records.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

An estimate for a hospital cannot remove fire-rated emergency Cable or selective protection merely to meet an initial budget target.

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Safety requirements are not optional allowances.
\- Check fault duty and disconnection.
\- Design for safe maintenance.
\- Critical loads need special rules.
\- Competent verification completes the design.

\# Chapter Summary

\- Electrical Estimation converts scope and design data into ratings, quantities, labour and cost.
\- Connected Load is the sum of installed input ratings within a defined boundary.
\- Maximum Demand is the highest interval-average load over a stated period.
\- Demand Factor = Maximum Demand / Connected Load and is normally not greater than 1.
\- Diversity Factor = sum of individual Maximum Demands / coincident group Maximum Demand and is normally at least 1.
\- Load Factor = Average Load / Maximum Demand over the same period.
\- Low Power Factor increases Current, loss, Voltage drop and kVA demand for the same kW.
\- Residential and commercial demand allowances must come from approved rules or justified data.
\- Preliminary load Current does not alone determine Cable size.
\- Cable selection also checks derating, Voltage drop, fault withstand and protection.
\- Protective-device selection checks load, starting, fault Current, breaking capacity and selectivity.
\- Electrical Energy in kWh equals kW multiplied by operating hours for constant load.
\- Electricity bills may include Energy, demand, fixed, PF, time-of-use and regulatory components.
\- Tariffs must be verified from current official publications.
\- Energy Management uses baselines, EnPIs, measurement, action and verification.
\- Conservation must not compromise safety, comfort, quality or required service.
\- Efficient equipment should be evaluated at actual load and by lifecycle cost.
\- A controlled estimate proceeds from scope and load to design, take-off, pricing and review.
\- Common mistakes include mixed units, double diversity, ignored efficiency, starting and harmonics.
\- Safety and verification requirements must remain in the estimate.
