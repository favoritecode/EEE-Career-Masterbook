\## Introduction



Electrical Power, Electrical Energy and Efficiency are fundamental concepts of Electrical and Electronic Engineering (EEE). These concepts explain how quickly electrical energy is transferred, how much energy an electrical device consumes and how effectively a system converts input energy into useful output energy.



From household appliances to industrial motors, generators, transformers and power plants, every electrical system has a power rating, consumes energy and operates with a certain efficiency.



This chapter explains Electrical Power, Energy, Efficiency, Energy Consumption and Billing in a simple, practical and engineering-oriented manner. It is intended for university students, diploma students, technical job preparation, competitive examinations and self-learning.

\## Topic 01: Electrical Power



\### Definition



Electrical Power is the rate at which electrical energy is transferred, converted or consumed in an electrical circuit.



Mathematically,



\*\*P = W / t\*\*



Where:



\- P = Electrical Power in Watt (W)

\- W = Electrical Energy or Work in Joule (J)

\- t = Time in Second (s)



For a DC circuit,



\*\*P = VI\*\*



Where:



\- V = Voltage in Volt (V)

\- I = Current in Ampere (A)



Therefore,



\*\*1 Watt = 1 Joule / 1 Second\*\*



\---



\### Importance



Electrical Power indicates how quickly an electrical device transfers or converts energy. The power rating of equipment helps engineers select suitable sources, cables, switches, protective devices and control components.



Electrical Power is important for:



\- Selecting electrical equipment and component ratings.

\- Calculating current demand.

\- Designing cables and protective devices.

\- Estimating energy consumption.

\- Comparing the operating capacity of electrical devices.

\- Troubleshooting overloaded circuits and equipment.



\---



\### Main Concepts



The basic DC power formula is:



\*\*P = VI\*\*



Using Ohm's Law, V = IR, two additional power formulas can be obtained:



\*\*P = I²R\*\*



\*\*P = V² / R\*\*



These formulas should be selected according to the known circuit quantities.



Common power units include:



\- Milliwatt (mW) = 10⁻³ W

\- Watt (W)

\- Kilowatt (kW) = 10³ W

\- Megawatt (MW) = 10⁶ W



For an electrical load, a higher power rating generally means that the device transfers or consumes energy at a faster rate under its rated operating conditions.



Example:



If a 12 V lamp draws 2 A:



\*\*P = VI\*\*



\*\*P = 12 × 2 = 24 W\*\*



\---



\### Real-Life Applications



\- Lamps are rated in Watt.

\- Electric motors are rated in Watt or Kilowatt.

\- Generators and transformers have specified capacity ratings.

\- Power supplies must provide sufficient power for connected loads.

\- Cable and circuit-breaker selection depends partly on load power and current.

\- Power measurement helps identify overloaded or faulty equipment.



\---



\### Key Takeaways



\- Electrical Power is the rate of electrical energy transfer.

\- Symbol: P

\- SI Unit: Watt (W)

\- Basic DC Formula: P = VI

\- Resistive Power Formulas: P = I²R and P = V²/R

\- 1 Watt = 1 Joule per Second.

\## Topic 02: Electrical Energy



\### Definition



Electrical Energy is the total electrical work performed or energy transferred by an electrical system over a period of time.



Mathematically,



\*\*W = Pt\*\*



Where:



\- W = Electrical Energy

\- P = Electrical Power

\- t = Operating Time



For a constant-voltage DC circuit,



\*\*W = VIt\*\*



\---



\### Importance



Electrical Energy determines the total amount of electrical work performed or electricity consumed over time. Electricity billing is based mainly on energy consumption rather than only on the instantaneous power rating of a device.



Electrical Energy is important for:



\- Calculating household and industrial electricity consumption.

\- Estimating operating cost.

\- Selecting batteries and backup systems.

\- Evaluating energy efficiency.

\- Planning power generation and energy management.

\- Comparing the operating cost of electrical equipment.



\---



\### Main Concepts



The SI unit of Energy is Joule (J).



In practical electrical systems, energy is commonly measured in:



\- Watt-hour (Wh)

\- Kilowatt-hour (kWh)

\- Megawatt-hour (MWh)



The relationships between these units are:



\*\*1 Wh = 3600 J\*\*



\*\*1 kWh = 1000 Wh\*\*



\*\*1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ J\*\*



One Kilowatt-hour is commonly called one unit of electrical energy for billing purposes.



Example:



If a 100 W lamp operates for 5 hours:



\*\*Energy = Power × Time\*\*



\*\*Energy = 100 W × 5 h = 500 Wh\*\*



\*\*Energy = 0.5 kWh\*\*



\---



\### Real-Life Applications



\- Energy meters record electricity consumption in kWh.

\- Battery capacity helps determine backup duration.

\- Solar energy systems are evaluated using daily or monthly energy production.

\- Factories monitor energy consumption to control operating cost.

\- Energy-efficient appliances reduce total electricity consumption.

\- Utility companies use energy data for billing and system planning.



\---



\### Key Takeaways



\- Electrical Energy is Power multiplied by Time.

\- Formula: W = Pt

\- SI Unit: Joule (J)

\- Practical Unit: Kilowatt-hour (kWh)

\- 1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ J.

\- One kWh is commonly called one unit of electricity.

\## Topic 03: Relationship Between Power and Energy



\### Definition



Power is the rate at which energy is transferred, while Energy is the total amount transferred over a period of time.



The relationship is:



\*\*Energy = Power × Time\*\*



Or,



\*\*W = Pt\*\*



\---



\### Importance



Understanding the relationship between Power and Energy prevents a common engineering mistake: confusing a device's power rating with its total energy consumption.



This relationship is important for:



\- Estimating operating time and energy use.

\- Comparing appliances with different power ratings.

\- Calculating battery backup requirements.

\- Estimating electricity cost.

\- Evaluating energy-saving opportunities.



\---



\### Main Concepts



Power tells us how fast energy is being transferred. Energy tells us how much has been transferred in total.



| Quantity | Meaning | Common Formula | Unit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Power | Rate of energy transfer | P = W/t | Watt (W) |
| Energy | Total energy transferred | W = Pt | Joule (J) or kWh |



A high-power device may consume less energy than a low-power device if it operates for a much shorter time.



Example:



A 2 kW heater operates for 30 minutes:



\*\*Time = 0.5 h\*\*



\*\*Energy = 2 kW × 0.5 h = 1 kWh\*\*



A 100 W lamp operates for 10 hours:



\*\*Energy = 0.1 kW × 10 h = 1 kWh\*\*



Although the devices have very different power ratings, they consume the same energy in these operating periods.



\---



\### Real-Life Applications



\- Estimating how long a battery can operate a load.

\- Comparing daily appliance usage.

\- Scheduling industrial equipment to reduce energy consumption.

\- Calculating solar energy production from average power and operating time.

\- Understanding why operating time affects electricity bills.



\---



\### Key Takeaways



\- Power is a rate; Energy is a total quantity.

\- Energy = Power × Time.

\- Watt and Kilowatt are units of Power.

\- Joule, Wh and kWh are units of Energy.

\- Both Power rating and operating time affect Energy consumption.

\## Topic 04: Efficiency



\### Definition



Efficiency is the ratio of useful output Power or Energy to total input Power or Energy.



Mathematically,



\*\*Efficiency, η = Useful Output / Total Input\*\*



As a percentage,



\*\*η = (Useful Output / Total Input) × 100%\*\*



Efficiency may be calculated using either Power or Energy, provided that the input and output quantities are compared consistently.



\---



\### Importance



No practical energy-conversion system is perfectly efficient. Some input energy is always converted into unwanted forms such as heat, sound, vibration or frictional loss.



Efficiency is important for:



\- Reducing electrical energy consumption.

\- Lowering operating cost.

\- Minimizing heat and other losses.

\- Selecting motors, transformers and power supplies.

\- Improving equipment reliability.

\- Supporting energy conservation and environmental goals.



\---



\### Main Concepts



The basic energy balance is:



\*\*Input = Useful Output + Losses\*\*



Therefore,



\*\*Losses = Input − Useful Output\*\*



Efficiency has no physical unit because it is a ratio. It is normally expressed as a percentage.



For practical equipment:



\*\*Efficiency < 100%\*\*



Example:



If a motor receives 1000 W of electrical input power and delivers 850 W of mechanical output power:



\*\*η = (850 / 1000) × 100%\*\*



\*\*η = 85%\*\*



The power loss is:



\*\*Loss = 1000 − 850 = 150 W\*\*



Common causes of loss include:



\- Electrical resistance

\- Magnetic loss

\- Mechanical friction

\- Windage

\- Unwanted heat and sound



\---



\### Real-Life Applications



\- Comparing electrical motors of the same output rating.

\- Evaluating transformer performance.

\- Selecting efficient power supplies and inverters.

\- Estimating battery-system losses.

\- Reducing industrial energy cost.

\- Identifying overheating or abnormal equipment loss.



\---



\### Key Takeaways



\- Efficiency compares useful output with total input.

\- Symbol: η

\- Efficiency is normally expressed as a percentage.

\- Input = Useful Output + Losses.

\- Practical Efficiency is always less than 100%.

\- Higher Efficiency means lower loss for the same useful output.

\## Topic 05: Energy Consumption and Billing



\### Definition



Energy Consumption is the total electrical energy used by one or more loads over a specified period. Electricity billing is the process of calculating the cost of supplied electrical energy and other applicable charges.



The basic energy calculation is:



\*\*Energy (kWh) = Power (kW) × Time (h)\*\*



The basic energy-charge calculation is:



\*\*Energy Charge = Energy Consumed × Tariff Rate\*\*



\---



\### Importance



Understanding Energy Consumption and Billing helps consumers and engineers estimate operating costs, identify high-energy loads and plan energy-saving measures.



It is important for:



\- Estimating monthly electricity bills.

\- Selecting energy-efficient appliances.

\- Monitoring household and industrial consumption.

\- Planning load operation.

\- Detecting unusual or excessive energy use.

\- Evaluating the financial benefit of energy-saving projects.



\---



\### Main Concepts



Electrical Energy meters commonly record energy consumption in Kilowatt-hour (kWh).



\*\*1 Unit of Electricity = 1 kWh\*\*



Example:



A 1.5 kW air conditioner operates for 4 hours per day for 30 days.



Daily energy consumption:



\*\*Energy = 1.5 × 4 = 6 kWh/day\*\*



Monthly energy consumption:



\*\*Energy = 6 × 30 = 180 kWh\*\*



If the assumed energy rate is 10 currency units per kWh:



\*\*Energy Charge = 180 × 10 = 1800 currency units\*\*



This is only the basic energy charge. An actual electricity bill may also include:



\- Fixed charges

\- Demand charges

\- Meter charges

\- Taxes or duties

\- Power-factor penalties or incentives

\- Slab-based or time-of-use tariff adjustments



Actual tariffs and billing rules depend on the utility, consumer category and applicable regulations.



\---



\### Real-Life Applications



\- Calculating the monthly operating cost of appliances.

\- Comparing conventional and energy-efficient equipment.

\- Monitoring factory energy use by department or machine.

\- Scheduling high-power loads during suitable tariff periods.

\- Verifying energy-meter readings and bill calculations.

\- Estimating savings from solar systems or efficient motors.



\---



\### Key Takeaways



\- Energy billing is commonly based on kWh consumption.

\- 1 Unit of Electricity = 1 kWh.

\- Energy = Power × Operating Time.

\- Energy Charge = kWh × Tariff Rate.

\- Actual bills may contain additional charges and tariff adjustments.

\- Lower operating time or lower power demand reduces energy consumption.

\## Topic 06: Practical Applications of Power and Energy



\### Definition



Practical Applications of Power and Energy involve using power ratings, operating time, energy consumption and efficiency to select, operate, measure and troubleshoot real electrical equipment.



These applications connect fundamental formulas with household, commercial and industrial engineering decisions.



\---



\### Importance



Power and Energy calculations are not limited to written examinations. They are used daily by engineers and technicians when selecting equipment, calculating loads, estimating cost and improving system performance.



Practical understanding is important for:



\- Equipment selection and rating.

\- Load estimation.

\- Battery and backup-system design.

\- Renewable-energy planning.

\- Energy auditing.

\- Preventive maintenance and troubleshooting.



\---



\### Main Concepts



\#### 1. Appliance Power Rating



The rated power indicates the expected rate of energy consumption or conversion under specified operating conditions.



\#### 2. Load Calculation



The total connected load is estimated by adding the rated power of connected equipment. Actual maximum demand may be lower because all loads may not operate simultaneously.



\#### 3. Cable and Protection Selection



Power and Voltage help determine load Current. Current is then used with applicable engineering standards to select cables, switches and protective devices.



For a DC load:



\*\*I = P / V\*\*



\#### 4. Battery Backup Estimation



Load Power and required operating time are used to estimate the Energy needed from a battery or uninterruptible power supply.



\#### 5. Energy Efficiency Improvement



High-efficiency motors, efficient lighting, proper equipment sizing and reduced idle operation can lower energy consumption without reducing useful output.



\#### 6. Measurement and Troubleshooting



Unexpectedly high input Power may indicate overloading, mechanical problems, low efficiency or an electrical fault. Power and Energy measurements support condition monitoring and energy audits.



\---



\### Real-Life Applications



\- Selecting a suitable power supply for electronic equipment.

\- Estimating the load of a home, office or factory.

\- Choosing a UPS for computers and communication systems.

\- Estimating solar-panel energy production.

\- Comparing LED lamps with conventional lamps.

\- Evaluating motor input Power, output Power and Efficiency.

\- Identifying unnecessary standby and idle energy consumption.



\---



\### Key Takeaways



\- Equipment ratings guide safe selection and operation.

\- Load Power and Voltage help determine Current.

\- Power and operating time determine Energy consumption.

\- Efficiency affects input requirement and operating cost.

\- Practical billing depends on energy use and the applicable tariff.

\- Measurement is essential for troubleshooting and energy management.
