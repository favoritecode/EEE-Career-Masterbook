\# Reference Notes

\- Electrical Engineering normally uses j = √−1 because i commonly represents instantaneous Current.

\- The Imaginary Part of a + jb is the coefficient b, not the complete term jb.

\- Rectangular Form is normally preferred for addition and subtraction, while Polar Form is normally preferred for multiplication and division.

\- Rectangular-to-Polar conversion requires a correct quadrant check using the signs of both components.

\- Polar-to-Rectangular conversion uses a = r cos θ and b = r sin θ.

\- Calculator degree and radian modes must match the angle convention used in the calculation.

\- Do not add Polar magnitudes directly when phase angles differ; first convert the Phasors to Rectangular Form.

\- A Phasor represents a sinusoidal quantity at a specified common frequency and does not directly represent a switching transient.

\- RMS and Peak Phasor magnitudes are both possible, but one convention must be stated and used consistently.

\- When using a cosine reference, convert a sine waveform by subtracting 90° from its phase angle.

\- Phasors of different frequencies cannot be combined directly into one constant Phasor result.

\- In standard Complex Impedance notation, +jX represents Inductive Reactance and −jX represents Capacitive Reactance.

\- Chapter-12 applies these Complex Number and Phasor methods to Impedance, Series RLC Circuits, phase relationships, Power Factor and the Power Triangle.
