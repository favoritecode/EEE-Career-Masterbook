\# Chapter 04

\# Series, Parallel and Series-Parallel Circuits

\---

\## Introduction

An Electric Circuit provides a complete path through which electric current can flow. Electrical sources, conductors, loads, switches and protective devices are connected in different arrangements to perform useful work safely and efficiently.

Series, Parallel and Series-Parallel Circuits are the fundamental connection types used in Electrical and Electronic Engineering (EEE). Their arrangement determines how Voltage, Current, Resistance and Power behave throughout a system.

This chapter explains Electric Circuits, Open Circuits, Closed Circuits, Short Circuits and the analysis of Series, Parallel and Series-Parallel Circuits in a simple, practical and engineering-oriented manner. It is intended for university students, diploma students, technical job preparation, competitive examinations and self-learning.

\---

After completing this chapter, you will be able to:

\- Explain the basic structure and operation of an Electric Circuit.
\- Differentiate between Open, Closed and Short Circuit conditions.
\- Identify Series, Parallel and Series-Parallel connections.
\- Calculate equivalent Resistance in Series and Parallel Circuits.
\- Explain how Voltage and Current behave in different circuit arrangements.
\- Compare the advantages and limitations of Series and Parallel Circuits.
\- Simplify basic Series-Parallel resistor networks.
\- Recognize practical circuit arrangements in household, industrial and electronic systems.
\- Apply circuit safety principles during measurement and troubleshooting.

\## Topic 01: Electric Circuit

\### Definition

An Electric Circuit is an interconnection of electrical components that provides one or more paths for electric current and allows electrical energy to be transferred from a source to a load.

A basic circuit normally contains:

\- An electrical source
\- Conducting wires
\- A load
\- A control device
\- A protective device when required

For continuous current to flow, the circuit must have a complete conducting path and a Potential Difference supplied by a source.

\---

\### Importance

Electric Circuits form the foundation of all electrical and electronic systems. Understanding circuit structure is necessary for:

\- Designing electrical installations
\- Connecting electrical equipment correctly
\- Calculating Voltage, Current, Resistance and Power
\- Selecting conductors and protective devices
\- Testing and troubleshooting electrical faults
\- Operating equipment safely
\- Studying advanced circuit analysis

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Source

The source supplies electrical energy and establishes a Potential Difference in the circuit.

\*\*Examples\*\*

\- Battery
\- DC power supply
\- Generator
\- Solar cell

\#### 2. Conducting Path

Conducting wires connect the circuit elements and provide a path for current. Copper and aluminium are commonly used conductors.

\#### 3. Load

A load receives electrical energy and converts it into another useful form.

\*\*Examples\*\*

\- Lamp: Electrical Energy to Light and Heat
\- Motor: Electrical Energy to Mechanical Energy
\- Heater: Electrical Energy to Heat

\#### 4. Control Device

A switch, relay or contactor controls the conducting path and therefore controls the operation of the load.

\#### 5. Protective Device

A fuse or circuit breaker disconnects the circuit when abnormal Current or a fault occurs.

\#### 6. Basic Circuit Quantities

The behavior of a circuit is described using:

\- Voltage (V)
\- Current (I)
\- Resistance (R)
\- Power (P)
\- Energy (E)

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

Electric Circuits are used in:

\- Household lighting and socket circuits
\- Mobile phone chargers
\- Motor control panels
\- Automotive electrical systems
\- Communication equipment
\- Solar power systems
\- Industrial automation
\- Medical and laboratory equipment

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- An Electric Circuit transfers electrical energy from a source to a load.
\- A circuit requires a conducting path and a Potential Difference.
\- Sources supply Energy, while loads convert Energy.
\- Switches control circuits and protective devices interrupt fault Current.
\- Voltage, Current, Resistance, Power and Energy describe circuit operation.

\## Topic 02: Open Circuit

\### Definition

An Open Circuit is a circuit in which the conducting path is broken or incomplete. Because there is no complete path, Current cannot flow through the entire circuit.

For an ideal Open Circuit:

\*\*I = 0 A\*\*

The Resistance across the open path is ideally infinite.

\---

\### Importance

Understanding Open Circuits is important because an open condition may be intentional or caused by a fault.

An Open Circuit can result from:

\- An open switch
\- A blown fuse
\- A broken wire
\- A loose terminal
\- A disconnected component
\- A burned-out lamp filament
\- A failed PCB track or connector

Correctly identifying an open condition is essential during circuit troubleshooting.

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Current in an Open Circuit

No continuous Current flows through an ideal Open Circuit because the conducting path is incomplete.

\#### 2. Voltage Across an Open Point

A Voltage may exist across the open points even though Current is zero. Therefore, an Open Circuit must not automatically be considered safe or de-energized.

\#### 3. Intentional Open Circuit

Opening a switch intentionally stops Current and turns a load off.

\#### 4. Fault Open Circuit

A broken conductor, loose connection or failed component can interrupt Current unexpectedly and stop equipment operation.

\#### 5. Testing an Open Circuit

An open condition can be located using:

\- Voltage measurement on an energized circuit by a trained person
\- Continuity or Resistance measurement on a de-energized circuit
\- Visual inspection of wires, terminals and components

Normal safety procedures and appropriate test equipment must always be followed.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

Open Circuit conditions are found in:

\- Wall switches in the OFF position
\- Protective interlocks
\- Emergency stop circuits
\- Blown fuses
\- Broken extension cables
\- Disconnected battery terminals
\- Open relay and contactor contacts

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- An Open Circuit has an incomplete conducting path.
\- Current in an ideal Open Circuit is zero.
\- Voltage may still be present across the open points.
\- Open conditions may be intentional or caused by faults.
\- Continuity testing should normally be performed on a de-energized circuit.

\## Topic 03: Closed Circuit

\### Definition

A Closed Circuit is a circuit with a complete conducting path that allows electric current to flow from the source, through the load and back to the source.

When a switch is closed in a correctly connected circuit, the load can receive electrical energy and operate.

\---

\### Importance

A Closed Circuit represents the normal operating condition of many electrical systems. Understanding this condition helps students and technicians:

\- Explain normal Current flow
\- Determine whether a load should operate
\- Apply Ohm's Law and Power formulas
\- Trace Current paths in schematics
\- Compare normal operation with open and short faults
\- Test switches, relays and control circuits

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Complete Path

A Closed Circuit has no intentional break in the required Current path.

\#### 2. Current Flow

Current flows when a source provides Voltage and the closed path has finite Resistance.

For an ohmic circuit:

\*\*I = V / R\*\*

\#### 3. Load Operation

The load converts electrical energy into light, heat, motion, sound or another useful form while Current flows.

\#### 4. Closed Switch

An ideal closed switch has approximately zero Resistance and a very small Voltage drop.

\#### 5. Normal and Abnormal Closed Paths

A properly closed path through a load is a normal condition. A nearly zero-Resistance path that bypasses the intended load is a Short Circuit and is dangerous.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

Closed Circuits are present when:

\- A room light is switched ON
\- A fan or motor is operating
\- A battery powers a torch lamp
\- A relay energizes a control load
\- A charger supplies Current to a battery
\- A heating element produces heat

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- A Closed Circuit has a complete Current path.
\- Current flows when Voltage is applied across a finite Resistance.
\- A closed switch permits normal load operation.
\- A properly closed circuit must include the intended load.
\- Closed Circuit and Short Circuit do not mean the same condition.

\## Topic 04: Short Circuit

\### Definition

A Short Circuit is an unintended or abnormal connection that provides a very low-Resistance path between points of different electrical potential. The low Resistance can allow an extremely high Current to flow.

According to Ohm's Law:

\*\*I = V / R\*\*

When R becomes very small, Current can become dangerously large. In a practical system, the Current is limited by source impedance, conductor impedance and other circuit impedance.

\---

\### Importance

Short Circuits are among the most serious electrical faults. They may cause:

\- Excessive Current
\- Rapid conductor heating
\- Insulation damage
\- Electric arc and arc flash
\- Fire
\- Equipment failure
\- Battery damage
\- Power interruption

Understanding Short Circuits is essential for selecting fuses, circuit breakers and other protective devices.

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Low-Resistance Fault Path

A Short Circuit bypasses all or part of the intended load and creates a path with much lower Resistance than normal.

\#### 2. Fault Current

The Current flowing during a Short Circuit is called Fault Current or Short-Circuit Current.

\#### 3. Common Causes

Short Circuits may be caused by:

\- Damaged insulation
\- Incorrect wiring
\- Loose conductive objects
\- Moisture or contamination
\- Failed components
\- Conductors touching each other
\- Wrong meter connection

\#### 4. Protective Devices

Fuses and circuit breakers are designed to interrupt excessive Current. Their interrupting capacity must be suitable for the available Fault Current.

\#### 5. Ammeter Safety

An ammeter has very low internal Resistance and must be connected in series. Connecting it directly across a Voltage source can create a Short Circuit.

\#### 6. Short Circuit vs Overload

An Overload is excessive Current through the normal load path. A Short Circuit is a low-impedance fault path that commonly produces a much higher Current.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

Short-Circuit knowledge is applied in:

\- Fuse and circuit-breaker selection
\- Power-system protection
\- Battery protection circuits
\- Industrial switchgear
\- Motor control panels
\- Cable and busbar design
\- Electrical safety studies
\- Fault investigation

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- A Short Circuit creates an abnormal low-Resistance path.
\- Short-Circuit Current can be extremely high and dangerous.
\- Fuses and circuit breakers interrupt excessive Fault Current.
\- Short Circuit and Overload are different fault conditions.
\- An ammeter must never be connected directly across a Voltage source.

\## Topic 05: Series Circuit

\### Definition

A Series Circuit is a circuit in which components are connected end-to-end, creating only one path for electric current.

Because there is only one Current path, the same Current flows through every component.

\---

\### Importance

Series Circuit analysis is important because it introduces fundamental rules for Current, Voltage and equivalent Resistance. It is used to:

\- Calculate total Resistance
\- Determine Voltage drops
\- Apply the Voltage Divider principle
\- Analyze basic sensor and control circuits
\- Understand the effect of an open component
\- Simplify part of a complex network

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Current Rule

The same Current flows through all components in a Series Circuit.

\*\*I total = I₁ = I₂ = I₃ = ...\*\*

\#### 2. Voltage Rule

The source Voltage equals the sum of all individual Voltage drops.

\*\*V total = V₁ + V₂ + V₃ + ...\*\*

\#### 3. Equivalent Resistance

The total Resistance of resistors connected in series is the sum of their individual Resistances.

\*\*R total = R₁ + R₂ + R₃ + ...\*\*

Therefore, adding a resistor in series increases the total Resistance.

\#### 4. Voltage Division

The Voltage across a series resistor is proportional to its Resistance.

For resistor R₁:

\*\*V₁ = V total × R₁ / R total\*\*

The largest Resistance receives the largest Voltage drop when the same Current flows through all resistors.

\#### 5. Power in a Series Circuit

Total Power equals the sum of the Power absorbed by the individual loads.

\*\*P total = P₁ + P₂ + P₃ + ...\*\*

\#### 6. Effect of an Open Component

If one component opens, the only Current path is broken and all components in that series path stop operating.

\#### 7. Example

If R₁ = 2 Ω, R₂ = 3 Ω and R₃ = 5 Ω are connected in series:

\*\*R total = 2 + 3 + 5 = 10 Ω\*\*

With a 20 V source:

\*\*I = V / R total = 20 / 10 = 2 A\*\*

The Voltage drops are 4 V, 6 V and 10 V respectively, and their sum is 20 V.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

Series connections are used in:

\- Voltage Divider Circuits
\- Current-limiting resistors with LEDs
\- Fuse connections
\- Ammeter connections
\- Battery cells connected for higher Voltage
\- Control and safety interlock loops
\- Some decorative light strings

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- A Series Circuit has one Current path.
\- The same Current flows through all series components.
\- Total Resistance is the sum of individual Resistances.
\- Source Voltage equals the sum of Voltage drops.
\- An open component interrupts the entire series path.

\## Topic 06: Parallel Circuit

\### Definition

A Parallel Circuit is a circuit in which components are connected across the same two nodes, creating two or more paths for electric current.

Because every branch is connected across the same two nodes, the same Voltage appears across every branch.

\---

\### Importance

Parallel Circuits are widely used because loads can receive the full supply Voltage and operate independently. Understanding Parallel Circuits is essential for:

\- Household and commercial wiring
\- Branch-circuit analysis
\- Current distribution
\- Equivalent Resistance calculation
\- Current Divider calculations
\- Independent control of multiple loads
\- Fault and maintenance analysis

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Voltage Rule

The Voltage across every parallel branch is equal to the supply Voltage.

\*\*V total = V₁ = V₂ = V₃ = ...\*\*

\#### 2. Current Rule

The total Current is the sum of the branch Currents.

\*\*I total = I₁ + I₂ + I₃ + ...\*\*

A lower-Resistance branch carries more Current when all branches have the same Voltage.

\#### 3. Equivalent Resistance

For parallel resistors:

\*\*1 / R total = 1 / R₁ + 1 / R₂ + 1 / R₃ + ...\*\*

For two resistors:

\*\*R total = R₁R₂ / (R₁ + R₂)\*\*

The equivalent Resistance of a parallel network is always lower than the smallest branch Resistance.

\#### 4. Equal Resistors in Parallel

For n equal resistors, each having Resistance R:

\*\*R total = R / n\*\*

\#### 5. Current Division

Current divides inversely according to branch Resistance. For two parallel resistors:

\*\*I₁ = I total × R₂ / (R₁ + R₂)\*\*

\*\*I₂ = I total × R₁ / (R₁ + R₂)\*\*

\#### 6. Power in a Parallel Circuit

Total Power equals the sum of the Power absorbed by all branches.

\*\*P total = P₁ + P₂ + P₃ + ...\*\*

\#### 7. Effect of an Open Branch

If one branch opens, Current stops only in that branch. Other complete branches can continue operating.

\#### 8. Example

If 6 Ω and 3 Ω resistors are connected in parallel:

\*\*R total = (6 × 3) / (6 + 3) = 2 Ω\*\*

With a 12 V source, the branch Currents are 2 A and 4 A, giving a total Current of 6 A.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

Parallel connections are used in:

\- Household lighting and socket circuits
\- Commercial building wiring
\- Automotive loads
\- Industrial distribution panels
\- Battery cells connected for increased Current capacity
\- Electronic bias and load networks
\- Multiple appliances on one supply

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- A Parallel Circuit has two or more Current paths.
\- The same Voltage appears across every branch.
\- Total Current is the sum of all branch Currents.
\- Equivalent Resistance is lower than the smallest branch Resistance.
\- An open branch does not normally stop the other complete branches.

\## Topic 07: Series-Parallel Circuit

\### Definition

A Series-Parallel Circuit is a circuit containing both Series and Parallel connections in the same network.

Some components share one Current path, while other components form separate branches. These circuits are also called Combination Circuits.

\---

\### Importance

Most practical electrical and electronic systems cannot be represented by a single Series or Parallel connection. Series-Parallel analysis is important for:

\- Simplifying resistor networks
\- Calculating total Resistance
\- Finding source Current
\- Determining branch Currents and Voltage drops
\- Estimating component Power
\- Troubleshooting practical systems
\- Preparing for Kirchhoff's Laws and Network Analysis

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Identifying Series Components

Two components are in series when the same Current must flow through both and their common node has no other branch connected to it.

\#### 2. Identifying Parallel Components

Two components are in parallel when both of their terminals are connected to the same two nodes and therefore have the same Voltage.

\#### 3. Step-by-Step Reduction

A basic Series-Parallel Circuit can be simplified using the following method:

1\. Identify the simplest Series or Parallel group.
2\. Calculate its equivalent Resistance.
3\. Replace that group with its equivalent Resistance.
4\. Repeat until one total Resistance remains.
5\. Calculate source Current using Ohm's Law.
6\. Work backward to find branch Voltages and Currents.
7\. Verify Current, Voltage and Power relationships.

\#### 4. Example Network

Suppose R₁ = 4 Ω is in series with a parallel combination of R₂ = 6 Ω and R₃ = 3 Ω.

First, calculate the parallel equivalent:

\*\*R₂₃ = (6 × 3) / (6 + 3) = 2 Ω\*\*

Then calculate total Resistance:

\*\*R total = R₁ + R₂₃ = 4 + 2 = 6 Ω\*\*

If the source Voltage is 12 V:

\*\*I total = 12 / 6 = 2 A\*\*

The Voltage drop across R₁ is 8 V, leaving 4 V across both parallel branches. Therefore, I₂ = 4/6 A and I₃ = 4/3 A, and their sum is 2 A.

\#### 5. Verification

A correct solution should satisfy:

\- Series Current relationships
\- Parallel Voltage relationships
\- Current balance at branch points
\- Voltage balance around closed paths
\- Total Power supplied = Total Power absorbed

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

Series-Parallel Circuits are found in:

\- Household distribution systems
\- Automotive wiring harnesses
\- Motor control circuits
\- Electronic resistor networks
\- Battery packs
\- Lighting control systems
\- Sensor and measurement circuits
\- Industrial control panels

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- A Series-Parallel Circuit contains both connection types.
\- Series components carry the same Current.
\- Parallel components have the same Voltage.
\- Networks can be simplified step by step using equivalent Resistance.
\- Results should be verified using Current, Voltage and Power relationships.

\## Topic 08: Comparison Between Series and Parallel Circuits

\### Definition

Series and Parallel Circuits are distinguished by the way components are connected and by the resulting behavior of Current, Voltage and equivalent Resistance.

A Series Circuit provides one Current path, while a Parallel Circuit provides two or more branch paths.

\---

\### Importance

Comparing Series and Parallel Circuits helps engineers select the correct arrangement for a practical purpose. The connection type affects:

\- Load Voltage
\- Circuit Current
\- Equivalent Resistance
\- Independent operation
\- Fault behavior
\- Power distribution
\- Source and protection requirements

\---

\### Main Concepts

| Property | Series Circuit | Parallel Circuit |
|----------|----------------|------------------|
| Current Paths | One | Two or More |
| Current | Same Through Every Component | Divides Among Branches |
| Voltage | Divides Across Components | Same Across Every Branch |
| Equivalent Resistance | Sum of Individual Resistances | Lower Than the Smallest Branch Resistance |
| Adding a Resistor | Increases Total Resistance | Decreases Total Resistance |
| Open Component | Interrupts the Entire Series Path | Normally Affects Only the Open Branch |
| Independent Load Operation | Limited | Normally Possible |
| Common Use | Voltage Dividers and Control Loops | Building Wiring and Multiple Loads |

\#### Series Advantages

\- Simple connection
\- Same Current through all components
\- Useful for Voltage division
\- Useful for current-sensing and protective devices

\#### Series Limitations

\- One open component stops the complete path
\- Loads do not normally receive the full source Voltage
\- Independent control is difficult

\#### Parallel Advantages

\- Every branch receives the supply Voltage
\- Loads can operate independently
\- One open branch does not normally stop other branches

\#### Parallel Limitations

\- Total source Current increases as more loads are added
\- More conductors and branch protection may be required
\- Incorrect design can overload the source or feeder

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

The comparison is applied when designing:

\- Household and commercial wiring
\- Battery packs
\- Lighting systems
\- Measurement circuits
\- Motor control systems
\- Electronic circuits
\- Industrial distribution networks

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Series Circuits have one path; Parallel Circuits have multiple paths.
\- Current is common in Series, while Voltage is common in Parallel.
\- Series Resistance adds directly.
\- Parallel equivalent Resistance is lower than the smallest branch Resistance.
\- Parallel connection is normally preferred when loads require independent operation at rated Voltage.

\## Topic 09: Practical Applications of Electrical Circuits

\### Definition

Practical Electrical Circuits combine sources, conductors, loads, control devices and protective devices using Series, Parallel or Series-Parallel arrangements to perform useful functions safely.

Circuit arrangement is selected according to required Voltage, Current, Power, control, reliability and protection.

\---

\### Importance

Practical circuit knowledge connects theoretical calculations with real equipment. It helps engineers and technicians:

\- Read circuit diagrams
\- Select suitable circuit arrangements
\- Measure Voltage and Current correctly
\- Diagnose open and short faults
\- Prevent overload and equipment damage
\- Design reliable control and distribution systems
\- Apply electrical safety procedures

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Household Wiring

Household loads are connected mainly in parallel so that each appliance receives the rated supply Voltage and can operate independently. Switches and protective devices are connected in the appropriate series path with the controlled load or circuit.

\#### 2. Battery Connections

Battery cells can be arranged according to system requirements:

\- Series connection increases total Voltage.
\- Parallel connection increases available Current capacity and Energy capacity when correctly designed.
\- Series-Parallel connection can increase both Voltage and capacity.

Only compatible cells with suitable balancing and protection should be connected together.

\#### 3. Lighting Circuits

Modern building lights are generally connected in parallel for full rated Voltage and independent operation. Series resistors or electronic drivers may be used within LED circuits for Current control.

\#### 4. Measurement Instruments

\- Ammeter: Connected in series with the load.
\- Voltmeter: Connected in parallel across two points.
\- Ohmmeter: Normally used on a de-energized circuit.

Incorrect instrument connection can damage the meter or create a hazardous fault.

\#### 5. Control and Protection

Switches, fuses, circuit breakers, relay contacts, overload contacts and emergency-stop contacts are placed according to the intended control and protective function.

\#### 6. Fault Diagnosis

A systematic troubleshooting process includes:

1\. Understand the circuit diagram and expected operation.
2\. Isolate the supply when required and follow safety procedures.
3\. Inspect conductors, terminals and components.
4\. Measure Voltage, continuity or Resistance using the correct method.
5\. Locate the Open Circuit, Short Circuit or abnormal Resistance.
6\. Correct the fault and verify safe operation.

\#### 7. Design Verification

A practical design must consider:

\- Rated Voltage and Current
\- Power and Energy requirements
\- Conductor size and Voltage drop
\- Protective-device ratings
\- Available Short-Circuit Current
\- Environmental conditions
\- Maintenance and isolation
\- Applicable electrical codes and standards

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

Electrical Circuit principles are applied in:

\- Residential distribution boards
\- Industrial motor control centers
\- Solar photovoltaic systems
\- Battery and UPS systems
\- Automotive electrical systems
\- Process-control panels
\- Electronic devices and power supplies
\- Communication and security systems

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Practical systems commonly combine Series and Parallel arrangements.
\- Household loads are normally connected in parallel.
\- Ammeters connect in series, while Voltmeters connect in parallel.
\- Circuit protection must be selected for the expected load and Fault Current.
\- Safe troubleshooting requires correct measurement methods and isolation procedures.
\- Practical design must follow equipment ratings and applicable standards.

\# Chapter Summary

\- An Electric Circuit transfers electrical energy from a source to a load through a conducting path.
\- An Open Circuit has an incomplete path and ideally carries no Current.
\- A Closed Circuit has a complete path and permits normal Current flow.
\- A Short Circuit creates an abnormal low-Resistance path and can produce dangerous Fault Current.
\- A Series Circuit has one path, and the same Current flows through every component.
\- Series Resistance is the sum of individual Resistances.
\- A Parallel Circuit has multiple paths, and the same Voltage appears across every branch.
\- Parallel equivalent Resistance is lower than the smallest branch Resistance.
\- A Series-Parallel Circuit combines both connection types and can be reduced step by step.
\- Household loads are normally connected in parallel for rated Voltage and independent operation.
\- Ammeters are connected in series, while Voltmeters are connected in parallel.
\- Correct protection and safe measurement practices are essential in practical circuits.
