\# Chapter 21

\# Generation of Electrical Power

\---

\## Introduction

Electrical Power Generation converts available primary Energy into electrical Energy at a Power Plant or distributed generating system. The original Energy may be chemical, gravitational, nuclear, solar, wind, biological, tidal or geothermal. Most large conventional plants first produce mechanical rotation and then use a Synchronous Generator, while Solar Photovoltaic systems convert sunlight directly into DC electricity and use Power Electronic converters for grid connection.

No generating method is ideal for every location or duty. Engineers compare fuel and resource availability, capital cost, operating cost, response speed, Efficiency, reliability, environmental impact, safety, construction time and Grid requirements. A reliable Power System therefore uses an appropriate mix of generating technologies.

This chapter explains the main types of Power Plants, their Energy-conversion paths, load and plant performance factors, Bangladesh's generation mix, environmental considerations and essential Power-Plant safety practices.

\## Topic 01: Introduction to Electrical Power Generation

\### Definition

Electrical Power Generation is the process of converting primary Energy into electrical Energy in a controlled form suitable for local use or delivery through a Power System.

\---

\### Importance

Generation is the first functional stage of a conventional Power System. Its capacity, availability, cost and controllability directly influence Grid frequency, continuity of supply, electricity price and industrial productivity.

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Energy-Conversion Chain

A generating station normally follows this chain:

\[ \text{Primary Energy}\rightarrow\text{Intermediate Energy}\rightarrow\text{Mechanical Energy}\rightarrow\text{Electrical Energy} \]

In a Thermal Plant, fuel produces Heat, Heat produces steam, steam rotates a Turbine and the Turbine drives a Generator. In Solar PV, light is converted directly into DC electricity.

\#### 2. Generator Principle

Most rotating Power Plants use Electromagnetic Induction. Relative motion between magnetic Flux and conductors induces EMF. A prime mover supplies shaft torque to the Generator.

\#### 3. Prime Mover

A Prime Mover is the machine that drives the Generator. Examples include Steam Turbine, Water Turbine, Gas Turbine, Wind Turbine and Diesel Engine.

\#### 4. Generating Station and Power Plant

The terms are often used interchangeably. A complete station contains the prime mover, Generator, auxiliary systems, protection, control, Switchyard and supporting facilities.

\#### 5. Centralized and Distributed Generation

Centralized stations feed a transmission network at large scale. Distributed Generation is installed closer to loads, such as rooftop Solar PV, industrial Captive Generation and small Cogeneration plants.

\#### 6. Essential Performance Requirements

Generation must provide:

\- Adequate active Power for demand and losses
\- Frequency support and reserve
\- Voltage and Reactive-Power support
\- Reliability and safe operation
\- Economic and environmentally responsible Energy conversion

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

When evening demand rises, system operators schedule additional generation or storage response so that total generation continues to match total demand and Grid losses.

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Power Plants convert primary Energy into electricity.
\- Most large plants use a prime mover and Synchronous Generator.
\- Solar PV performs direct electro-optical conversion before inverter connection.
\- Generation and demand must remain balanced in real time.
\- A practical plant includes auxiliaries, control, protection and a Switchyard.

\## Topic 02: Sources of Electrical Energy

\### Definition

An Energy source is a naturally available or processed resource whose Energy can be converted into electricity.

\---

\### Importance

Source selection determines plant location, fuel security, cost, emissions, water requirement, response capability and long-term sustainability.

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Non-Renewable Sources

Coal, natural gas, oil and nuclear fuel are finite on a human time scale. Fossil fuels release stored chemical Energy by combustion; nuclear fuel releases Energy through controlled fission.

\#### 2. Renewable Sources

Solar radiation, wind, flowing water, sustainable Biomass, tides and geothermal Heat are replenished naturally. Renewable does not mean impact-free; land, materials, ecology and Grid integration still require engineering assessment.

\#### 3. Primary and Secondary Energy

Sunlight, coal, gas and flowing water are Primary Energy sources. Electricity and Hydrogen are Energy carriers produced from other sources and are therefore usually called Secondary Energy.

\#### 4. Dispatchable and Variable Sources

Dispatchable plants can adjust output when instructed within technical limits. Solar and Wind output depends on weather and is variable, although forecasting, storage, flexible generation and transmission improve integration.

\#### 5. Source Comparison

| Source | Renewable? | Typical Conversion | Main Constraint |
|---|---|---|---|
| Coal/Gas/Oil | No | Heat → Mechanical → Electrical | Fuel, emissions and cost |
| Nuclear | No | Fission Heat → Mechanical → Electrical | Safety, waste and high capital cost |
| Hydro | Yes | Water potential → Mechanical → Electrical | Suitable site and ecology |
| Solar PV | Yes | Light → Electrical | Daylight and weather variability |
| Wind | Yes | Wind kinetic → Mechanical → Electrical | Variable wind resource |
| Biomass | Renewable if sustainable | Chemical → Heat/Gas → Electrical | Feedstock and emissions control |
| Tidal | Yes | Tidal motion/head → Electrical | Limited sites and marine impact |
| Geothermal | Renewable with good reservoir management | Earth Heat → Mechanical → Electrical | Geological suitability |

\#### 6. Energy Mix

An Energy Mix combines sources to balance cost, reliability, domestic-resource security, environmental targets and operational flexibility.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

A country with limited domestic fuel may combine imported fuel, cross-border electricity, Solar, Wind and storage to reduce dependence on a single source.

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Energy sources may be renewable or non-renewable.
\- Electricity is normally an Energy carrier, not a primary source.
\- Dispatchability and variability are different characteristics.
\- Renewable systems also have site and environmental constraints.
\- A diversified mix can improve Energy security.

\## Topic 03: Conventional Power Plants

\### Definition

Conventional Power Plants are established large-scale generating technologies commonly based on fossil fuel, nuclear fission or large Hydropower. Classification varies by textbook; in this chapter Thermal, Hydro, Nuclear, Diesel and Gas-Turbine plants are grouped as conventional.

\---

\### Importance

These stations have historically supplied most Grid electricity and continue to provide dispatchable capacity, inertia, reserve and large unit output in many Power Systems.

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Thermal Power Plant

Main parts include fuel handling, Boiler or Steam Generator, Superheater, Steam Turbine, Alternator, Condenser, cooling system, feedwater system, flue-gas treatment and Switchyard.

Working sequence:

1\. Fuel Heat converts water into high-pressure steam.
2\. Steam expands through the Turbine.
3\. The Turbine drives the Alternator.
4\. Exhaust steam condenses and feedwater returns to the Boiler.

Advantages include large unit ratings and established technology. Limitations include fuel cost, emissions, cooling-water need, slow start for large steam units and significant auxiliary consumption.

\#### 2. Hydroelectric Power Plant

Main parts include reservoir or river intake, Dam where applicable, Trash Rack, Penstock, Surge Tank where required, Water Turbine, Generator, Draft Tube, Tailrace and Switchyard.

Water head produces flow through the Turbine. The Turbine drives the Generator, and water returns downstream. Hydro plants can start rapidly and may provide peaking, frequency control and storage. Limitations include site dependence, high civil cost, hydrological uncertainty and ecological/social impact.

Hydraulic Power is approximately:

\[ P=\rho gQH\eta \]

where ρ is water density, g is gravitational acceleration, Q is flow rate, H is net head and η is overall Efficiency.

\#### 3. Nuclear Power Plant

Main systems include Reactor, nuclear fuel, Moderator where required, Control Rods, Coolant circuit, Steam Generator or direct steam system, Turbine-Generator, Condenser, containment and engineered safety systems.

Controlled fission produces Heat. That Heat produces steam, which drives a Steam Turbine and Generator. Nuclear plants offer high Energy density and low operational carbon emissions, but require very high capital investment, rigorous safety, security, waste management and long project timelines.

\#### 4. Diesel Power Plant

A Compression-Ignition Engine converts fuel Energy into shaft Power and directly drives an Alternator. Supporting systems include fuel, air intake, exhaust, cooling, lubrication, starting, control and protection.

Diesel plants start quickly, occupy limited space and suit standby or remote duty. Their main limitations are high fuel and maintenance cost, noise, vibration and exhaust emissions.

\#### 5. Gas Turbine Power Plant

In an Open-Cycle Gas Turbine, a Compressor raises air pressure, fuel burns in a Combustion Chamber and hot gas expands through the Turbine. Part of Turbine Power drives the Compressor; the remainder drives the Generator.

Gas Turbines start rapidly and have high Power-to-weight ratio. Open-cycle Efficiency falls at high ambient temperature and part load. In a Combined-Cycle Plant, hot exhaust produces steam in a Heat-Recovery Steam Generator, increasing overall Efficiency through a Steam Turbine.

\#### 6. Conventional Plant Comparison

| Plant | Prime Mover | Start/Response | Typical Duty | Major Limitation |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Steam Thermal | Steam Turbine | Slow to moderate | Base/intermediate | Fuel emissions and water |
| Hydro | Water Turbine | Fast | Base, peak or storage | Site and hydrology |
| Nuclear | Steam Turbine | Generally slow | Base Load | Capital, safety and waste |
| Diesel | Diesel Engine | Fast | Standby/peak/remote | Fuel and maintenance cost |
| Gas Turbine | Gas Turbine | Fast | Peak/intermediate | Fuel cost and ambient derating |
| Combined Cycle | Gas + Steam Turbines | Moderate | Intermediate/base | Greater system complexity |

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

A hospital may use Grid supply as its normal source and a Diesel Generator for emergency backup, while a utility may use Combined-Cycle generation for efficient daily service and a fast Gas Turbine for sudden peaks.

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Thermal and Nuclear plants use steam to drive a Turbine.
\- Hydro output depends on water flow, head and Efficiency.
\- Diesel plants are useful for standby and isolated supply.
\- Gas Turbines provide fast response; Combined Cycle recovers exhaust Heat.
\- Plant selection depends on both technical duty and lifecycle economics.

\## Topic 04: Non-Conventional (Renewable) Power Plants

\### Definition

Renewable Power Plants convert naturally replenished Energy flows into electricity. In many traditional syllabi these are called non-conventional plants.

\---

\### Importance

Renewable generation can reduce fossil-fuel use and operational emissions, diversify supply and support distributed electrification, but it also introduces resource, land, storage and Grid-integration requirements.

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Solar Power Plant

A Solar PV Plant uses semiconductor cells to produce DC Power from sunlight. Modules form strings and arrays; Combiner Boxes, DC protection, inverters, Transformers, monitoring and Switchgear deliver Grid-compatible AC.

Advantages include modularity, no fuel and low operational emissions. Limitations include daytime/weather dependence, land or roof area, inverter replacement, soiling and the need for flexibility or storage at high penetration.

\#### 2. Wind Power Plant

Wind turns Rotor blades. The Rotor drives a Generator directly or through a Gearbox. Power Electronics regulate speed and Grid output; a Transformer raises Voltage.

Available Wind Power is:

\[ P_{wind}=\frac{1}{2}\rho Av^3 \]

Only part can be captured. Wind Energy is clean in operation, but output varies strongly with wind speed and requires a suitable site, Grid connection and environmental assessment.

\#### 3. Biomass Power Plant

Biomass may be burned in a Boiler, gasified into producer gas, or converted to Biogas by anaerobic digestion. The resulting steam or gas drives a Turbine or Engine-Generator.

It can use agricultural residue and provide dispatchable output, but sustainability depends on feedstock collection, moisture, land use, air-pollution control and replanting or waste-management practice.

\#### 4. Tidal Power Plant

Tidal Barrage systems use the water-level difference across an estuary, while tidal-stream systems use moving seawater. Tidal timing is highly predictable, but suitable sites are limited and marine construction and ecological impacts can be substantial.

\#### 5. Geothermal Power Plant

Dry-steam, Flash-Steam or Binary-Cycle plants use underground Heat. Binary plants transfer Heat to a secondary low-boiling fluid. Geothermal can provide steady output, but exploration risk, drilling cost, corrosion, scaling and site availability are important.

\#### 6. Renewable Plant Comparison

| Plant | Resource | Conversion | Dispatch Character |
|---|---|---|---|
| Solar PV | Sunlight | Direct electrical | Variable, daytime |
| Wind | Moving air | Turbine-Generator | Variable |
| Biomass | Organic material | Thermal/chemical then mechanical | Usually dispatchable |
| Tidal | Tidal head/current | Water Turbine-Generator | Variable but predictable |
| Geothermal | Earth Heat | Steam/binary Turbine | Often steady |

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

An industrial rooftop Solar system can reduce daytime Grid import, while a Biomass Cogeneration plant can use process residue to produce both electricity and useful Heat.

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Solar PV converts light directly into DC electricity.
\- Wind Power varies approximately with the cube of wind speed before control limits.
\- Biomass is renewable only when its feedstock cycle is sustainable.
\- Tidal output is predictable but site-specific.
\- Geothermal can provide steady Power where suitable Heat resources exist.

\---

\## Topic 05: Working Principle of Different Power Plants

\### Definition

The working principle of a Power Plant is the ordered Energy-conversion process by which its source produces controlled electrical output.

\---

\### Importance

Tracing the conversion path helps engineers identify losses, auxiliary requirements, control points, hazards and suitable efficiency improvements.

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Conversion Paths

| Plant | Energy-Conversion Path |
|---|---|
| Coal/Gas Steam Plant | Chemical → Thermal → Steam → Mechanical → Electrical |
| Nuclear Plant | Nuclear → Thermal → Steam → Mechanical → Electrical |
| Hydro Plant | Potential/Kinetic → Mechanical → Electrical |
| Diesel Plant | Chemical → Thermal/pressure → Mechanical → Electrical |
| Gas Turbine | Chemical → Thermal/kinetic gas → Mechanical → Electrical |
| Solar PV | Solar radiation → DC electrical → AC electrical |
| Wind Plant | Wind kinetic → Mechanical → Electrical |
| Biomass Plant | Chemical → Thermal/gas → Mechanical → Electrical |
| Tidal Plant | Tidal potential/kinetic → Mechanical → Electrical |
| Geothermal Plant | Earth Heat → Working-fluid Energy → Mechanical → Electrical |

\#### 2. Energy Balance

For a plant over a defined period:

\[ \eta=\frac{\text{Useful electrical Energy output}}{\text{Primary Energy input}}\times100\% \]

The denominator must be defined consistently. Renewable-resource assessment may use a different performance ratio from fuel-fired thermal Efficiency.

\#### 3. Auxiliary Power

Pumps, fans, compressors, conveyors, cooling systems, excitation and controls consume part of gross Generation.

\[ P_{net}=P_{gross}-P_{auxiliary} \]

\#### 4. Grid Interface

Rotating Generators require excitation, synchronization, protection and Voltage transformation. Inverter-based plants require DC collection, Maximum Power Point Tracking where applicable, inverter control, filtering, protection and Grid-code compliance.

\#### 5. Control of Output

Output may be controlled by fuel flow, steam valves, water gates, blade pitch, Generator torque, inverter control or resource curtailment. The method depends on plant technology.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

In a Combined-Cycle Plant, exhaust Heat that would leave an Open-Cycle Gas Turbine is recovered to produce steam and additional electricity, improving overall fuel utilization.

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Every plant can be understood as an Energy-conversion chain.
\- Gross output differs from net output because auxiliaries consume Power.
\- Thermal, mechanical and electrical losses reduce overall Efficiency.
\- Rotating and inverter-based generators use different Grid interfaces.
\- Clear system boundaries are essential when comparing Efficiency.

\---

\- \*\*Author\*\*: P. K. Nag
\- \*\*Relevant Chapter\*\*: Energy conversion, plant cycles and auxiliary systems
\- \*\*Supporting Text\*\*: Electric Power Generation, Transmission, and Distribution — Leonard L. Grigsby, editor — Generation principles and Grid interface

\## Topic 06: Base Load and Peak Load Power Plants

\### Definition

Base Load is the relatively continuous minimum system demand. Peak Load is the higher demand occurring for limited periods. Plants are scheduled according to their technical and economic suitability for these duties.

\---

\### Importance

Matching plant characteristics to demand reduces operating cost while maintaining adequate reserve, frequency control and reliability.

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Base-Load Plant

A Base-Load Plant operates for long periods at high output. Traditionally, efficient large Thermal, Nuclear, Combined-Cycle or suitable Hydro plants perform this duty. Actual scheduling depends on system conditions and economics.

\#### 2. Peak-Load Plant

A Peak-Load Plant starts or raises output rapidly for short demand peaks. Gas Turbines, reservoir Hydro, Pumped Storage, Diesel units and Battery Storage may serve this role.

\#### 3. Intermediate-Load Plant

Intermediate or Load-Following plants vary output during normal daily demand changes. Flexible Thermal, Combined-Cycle and Hydro units may be scheduled here.

\#### 4. Selection Factors

| Requirement | Base-Load Preference | Peak-Load Preference |
|---|---|---|
| Operating hours | High | Low |
| Running cost | Low per kWh | May be higher |
| Start time | Less critical | Very short |
| Capital cost | Can be high | Prefer lower fixed cost |
| Efficiency at rated output | Very important | Important but secondary to flexibility |
| Ramp rate | Moderate acceptable | Fast preferred |

\#### 5. Reserve

Operating Reserve covers unexpected loss of generation or forecast error. Spinning and fast-start resources contribute according to Grid rules.

\#### 6. Modern System Note

High Solar and Wind penetration can shift the net-load curve. Flexible generation, storage, interconnection and demand response then become more important than a fixed traditional classification.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

A fast Hydro or Gas-Turbine unit may increase output during the evening peak when lighting, cooling and household demand rise as Solar output falls.

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Base Load is continuous minimum demand, not a specific technology.
\- Peak plants must respond quickly and may run fewer hours.
\- Intermediate plants follow ordinary demand variation.
\- Reserve supports reliability after disturbances.
\- Plant scheduling changes with fuel prices, renewables and Grid constraints.

\## Topic 07: Load Curve and Load Duration Curve

\### Definition

A Load Curve plots Power demand against chronological time. A Load Duration Curve contains the same demand values arranged from highest to lowest, showing how long each load level is equaled or exceeded.

\---

\### Importance

These curves support plant sizing, Energy estimation, maintenance planning, generation scheduling, tariff analysis and Base/Peak capacity selection.

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Daily Load Curve

Demand is recorded through 24 hours in time order. It shows when peaks and valleys occur.

\#### 2. Monthly and Annual Curves

Longer curves reveal seasonal behavior, annual Peak Demand and plant utilization.

\#### 3. Energy from a Load Curve

The area under a Power-versus-time curve equals Energy:

\[ E=\int P(t)\,dt \]

For interval data:

\[ E\approx\sum P_i\Delta t_i \]

\#### 4. Average Load

\[ P_{avg}=\frac{E}{T} \]

where E is Energy during period T.

\#### 5. Constructing a Load Duration Curve

1\. Collect chronological load values.
2\. Arrange values from highest to lowest.
3\. Plot load against cumulative duration.
4\. Preserve the same total area and therefore the same Energy.

\#### 6. Curve Comparison

| Feature | Load Curve | Load Duration Curve |
|---|---|---|
| Time order | Preserved | Removed |
| Shows time of peak | Yes | No |
| Shows duration above load | Less direct | Yes |
| Area represents Energy | Yes | Yes |
| Useful for scheduling | Chronological operation | Capacity and utilization planning |

\#### 7. Worked Example

If load is 20 MW for 6 h, 12 MW for 10 h and 8 MW for 8 h:

\[ E=(20\times6)+(12\times10)+(8\times8)=304\text{ MWh} \]

\[ P_{avg}=304/24=12.67\text{ MW} \]

Peak Load is 20 MW. Sorting these blocks by magnitude forms the Load Duration Curve.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

A utility uses an annual Load Duration Curve to estimate how many hours a proposed generating unit would operate above a chosen demand level.

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- A Load Curve preserves chronological order.
\- A Load Duration Curve sorts demand from high to low.
\- Area under either curve represents Energy.
\- Average Load equals Energy divided by time.
\- Both curves are required for sound planning.

\## Topic 08: Capacity Factor, Plant Factor and Load Factor

\### Definition

Load Factor describes how steadily a load uses its peak demand. Capacity Factor and Plant Factor describe how much Energy a generating plant produces compared with defined maximum possible output.

\---

\### Importance

These factors are frequently used in examinations, planning, operation and cost analysis. Their definitions must be stated carefully because terminology can vary among textbooks and utilities.

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Load Factor

\[ \text{Load Factor}=\frac{\text{Average Load}}{\text{Maximum Demand}} \]

or

\[ \text{Load Factor}=\frac{\text{Energy in period}}{\text{Maximum Demand}\times\text{period hours}} \]

It is less than or equal to 1. A higher Load Factor indicates steadier use of system capacity.

\#### 2. Capacity Factor

\[ \text{Capacity Factor}=\frac{\text{Actual Energy generated}}{\text{Rated Capacity}\times\text{total period hours}} \]

It reflects resource availability, maintenance, dispatch, outages, curtailment and system demand.

\#### 3. Plant Factor

Many power-engineering texts use Plant Capacity Factor or Plant Factor for the same ratio as Capacity Factor. Some texts instead use the plant's maximum demand in the denominator. Therefore, always quote the adopted definition before calculation.

For this chapter:

\[ \text{Plant Factor}=\frac{\text{Actual Energy generated}}{\text{Plant Rated Capacity}\times\text{period hours}} \]

\#### 4. Plant Use Factor

Do not confuse Plant Factor with Plant Use Factor:

\[ \text{Plant Use Factor}=\frac{\text{Actual Energy generated}}{\text{Rated Capacity}\times\text{hours actually operated}} \]

\#### 5. Worked Example

A 100 MW plant generates 43,800 MWh in a 30-day period.

Total hours = 30 × 24 = 720 h.

\[ \text{Capacity Factor}=\frac{43,800}{100\times720}=0.6083=60.83\% \]

If it operated for only 600 h:

\[ \text{Plant Use Factor}=\frac{43,800}{100\times600}=73\% \]

\#### 6. Load-Factor Example

If a consumer uses 9,600 kWh in 30 days and Maximum Demand is 20 kW:

\[ \text{Load Factor}=\frac{9,600}{20\times720}=66.67\% \]

\#### 7. Interpretation

A low Capacity Factor is not automatically poor performance. A peaking plant may be intentionally operated for few hours, while a Wind or Solar plant is limited by resource availability.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

Engineers compare annual Energy and rated capacity to evaluate utilization, but they also examine availability, forced outages, dispatch instructions and resource conditions before judging performance.

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Load Factor relates Average Load to Maximum Demand.
\- Capacity Factor relates actual Energy to rated maximum possible Energy.
\- Plant Factor terminology varies; state the definition used.
\- Plant Use Factor counts only operating hours.
\- A factor must be interpreted according to plant duty and resource.

\---

\## Topic 09: Advantages and Disadvantages of Different Power Plants

\### Definition

Power-Plant comparison is the systematic evaluation of technical, economic, environmental and operational benefits and limitations of alternative technologies.

\---

\### Importance

Choosing only by capital cost or Efficiency can produce an unreliable or expensive system. Engineers must evaluate complete lifecycle and Power-System duty.

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Multi-Criteria Comparison

| Plant | Main Advantages | Main Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| Steam Thermal | Large proven units; fuel flexibility | Emissions, water use, slower start |
| Hydro | Fast response; high Efficiency; no combustion fuel | Site-limited; high civil work; ecological/social effects |
| Nuclear | High Energy density; low operational carbon emissions | High capital; long construction; safety and waste duties |
| Diesel | Fast start; compact; good standby source | High fuel/maintenance cost; noise and emissions |
| Gas Turbine | Rapid start; compact; useful for peaks | Lower Open-Cycle Efficiency; ambient-temperature sensitivity |
| Combined Cycle | High fuel Efficiency; flexible configurations | More complex; depends on reliable gas and water/cooling design |
| Solar PV | Modular; no fuel; low operating emissions | Variable; area and inverter/Grid requirements |
| Wind | No fuel; low operating emissions | Variable; site, visual, noise and wildlife concerns |
| Biomass | Dispatchable; can use residue | Feedstock logistics and emissions; sustainability risk |
| Tidal | Predictable resource | Expensive marine works; few sites; ecological impact |
| Geothermal | Often firm output; small surface footprint | Site and drilling risk; scaling/corrosion |

\#### 2. Economic Factors

Consider capital cost, financing, construction time, fuel cost, operation and maintenance, plant life, Capacity Factor, decommissioning and external environmental cost.

\#### 3. Technical Factors

Consider unit size, start time, ramp rate, minimum stable output, Efficiency, black-start capability, inertia, Reactive-Power capability, maintenance outage and fuel/resource security.

\#### 4. Environmental and Social Factors

Consider greenhouse gases, air pollutants, water withdrawal, thermal discharge, land, biodiversity, noise, waste, community displacement and occupational risk.

\#### 5. No Universal Winner

A plant suitable for continuous industrial Base Load may be unsuitable for emergency standby. Technology must match duty, location and system needs.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

A remote telecommunication site may prefer Solar plus Battery and backup generation, while a national Grid requires a broader portfolio of controllable, variable and reserve resources.

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Compare technologies on lifecycle, not one parameter.
\- Fast response and low running cost are different benefits.
\- Environmental impacts differ by technology and site.
\- Resource security is part of reliability.
\- A balanced portfolio usually performs better than a single technology.

\## Topic 10: Power Generation in Bangladesh — Basic Overview

\### Definition

Bangladesh's Power-Generation Sector is the combination of public, joint-venture and private generating plants, Captive and renewable systems, and cross-border imports that supply the national electricity network and local loads.

\---

\### Importance

EEE students in Bangladesh should understand the local fuel mix, major institutions, operational constraints and transition toward a more diversified and sustainable Power System.

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Institutional Structure

The Power Division provides government policy direction. Bangladesh Power Development Board (BPDB) generates electricity, purchases Power as a Single Buyer and performs other sector functions. Public generation companies, Independent Power Producers and joint ventures operate plants. Power Grid Bangladesh PLC operates the transmission Grid, while distribution utilities deliver electricity to consumers.

\#### 2. Generation Mix

Bangladesh uses natural gas, coal, liquid fuel, Hydropower, Solar and Wind, together with imported electricity. The mix changes as units enter service, retire, become unavailable or face fuel constraints.

According to BPDB's fuel-wise dashboard for July 2026, Grid-connected installed capacity was reported as 29,593 MW. The dashboard listed gas as the largest category, followed by coal and liquid fuels, with smaller shares from imports, Solar, Hydro and Wind. These figures are a dated operational snapshot, not a permanent value.

\#### 3. Public, Private and Joint-Venture Participation

Generation assets are operated by BPDB and public companies, private IPPs and joint ventures. Cross-border interconnections also contribute to supply. Installed capacity is different from available or generated Power at a particular time.

\#### 4. Hydropower and Renewable Development

Kaptai Hydroelectric Power Station is the country's principal large Hydro facility. Utility Solar, rooftop Solar, Solar Home Systems, Solar Irrigation, mini-grids and Wind projects support renewable development. SREDA coordinates sustainable Energy and renewable initiatives.

\#### 5. Key Engineering Challenges

\- Reliable domestic and imported fuel supply
\- Difference between installed, derated and available capacity
\- Seasonal and daily demand variation
\- Transmission constraints and Grid stability
\- Cost of imported Energy and foreign-exchange exposure
\- Integrating variable renewable generation
\- Improving plant Efficiency, dispatch and maintenance
\- Reducing emissions while maintaining affordability and reliability

\#### 6. Important Examination Note

Do not treat installed capacity as actual generation. Actual output depends on available capacity, fuel, maintenance, dispatch, transmission limits, weather and demand.

\#### 7. Data-Use Rule

Power-sector figures change frequently. For reports and job interviews, state the reference date and verify the latest BPDB, Power Division and SREDA publications.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

A planning engineer comparing new generation options must study not only national installed capacity but also hourly demand, fuel availability, plant outages, transmission capability and the location of new load centers.

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Bangladesh uses a mixed public, private and joint-venture generation structure.
\- Gas, coal and liquid fuels remain important, with renewable and imported Power also contributing.
\- Installed capacity is not equal to dependable output or annual Energy.
\- BPDB, Power Division and SREDA are essential official information sources.
\- Every numerical sector statement needs a date and source.

\## Topic 11: Environmental Impact of Power Generation

\### Definition

Environmental impact is any change to air, water, land, climate, ecosystems or communities caused by constructing, operating, fueling or retiring a Power Plant.

\---

\### Importance

Electricity supports development, but unmanaged impacts can harm health, ecosystems, water security and climate. Environmental control must therefore be part of plant design and operation.

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Air Emissions

Fossil-fuel plants may emit carbon dioxide, nitrogen oxides, sulfur oxides and particulate matter. Control methods include efficient combustion, low-NOx systems, Flue-Gas Desulfurization, particulate collectors, cleaner fuel and carbon-reduction strategies.

\#### 2. Greenhouse-Gas Impact

Operational emissions differ from lifecycle emissions. Construction, mining, fuel transport, manufacturing and decommissioning should be considered when comparing technologies.

\#### 3. Water and Thermal Pollution

Steam-cycle plants may withdraw water and reject Heat through condensers. Cooling towers, closed-cycle systems, treatment and discharge-temperature control reduce impact but do not eliminate water requirements.

\#### 4. Solid and Hazardous Waste

Coal ash, treatment sludge, used oil, contaminated filters, Solar modules, Batteries and nuclear waste require technology-specific handling and long-term management.

\#### 5. Land and Biodiversity

Dams alter rivers and habitats; Solar and Wind require suitable land or sea areas; mining and fuel transport disturb ecosystems. Careful siting, impact assessment and mitigation are essential.

\#### 6. Noise and Visual Impact

Turbines, Engines, cooling equipment and Wind Turbines may create noise. Acoustic enclosure, distance, barriers, balancing and maintenance reduce exposure.

\#### 7. Environmental Management Hierarchy

1\. Avoid impact through site and technology choice.
2\. Minimize unavoidable impact by design.
3\. Control and monitor emissions and discharge.
4\. Restore affected areas.
5\. Compensate residual impact where required.

\#### 8. Just Transition and Community

Engineering decisions should consider affordability, employment, public health, land rights, resettlement and fair access to reliable electricity.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

Continuous stack-emission monitoring and cooling-water temperature measurement help operators confirm compliance and detect deteriorating plant performance.

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Every generation technology has lifecycle impacts.
\- Fossil plants require air-pollution and carbon management.
\- Thermal plants can significantly affect water systems.
\- Renewable plants require responsible siting and end-of-life planning.
\- Avoidance and minimization are preferred before compensation.

\## Topic 12: Safety Practices in Power Plants

\### Definition

Power-Plant safety is the planned control of electrical, mechanical, thermal, pressure, chemical, radiation, fire and process hazards throughout operation and maintenance.

\---

\### Importance

Generating stations contain High Voltage, large rotating machines, stored Energy, hot surfaces, pressurized fluids, fuels and automatic control. A single uncontrolled Energy source can cause fatal injury or major plant damage.

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Hazard Identification and Permit to Work

Before work, identify the task, equipment boundaries, hazards, required isolation, competent persons, PPE, tests and restoration steps. Use the approved Permit-to-Work system.

\#### 2. Electrical Isolation

Disconnect every source, prevent reconnection, apply Lockout/Tagout according to site procedure, verify absence of Voltage with a correctly rated tester and apply earthing where required.

\#### 3. Mechanical and Process Isolation

Control rotation, pressure, steam, water, fuel, compressed air, gravity, hydraulic Energy and stored spring Energy. Electrical isolation alone may be insufficient.

\#### 4. High-Voltage and Arc-Flash Safety

Maintain approach boundaries, use interlocks and remote operation where designed, select Arc-Rated PPE from an approved assessment and never defeat protection without formal authorization.

\#### 5. Turbine and Rotating-Machine Safety

Keep guards installed, monitor vibration and Overspeed protection, control barring gear and shaft rotation, and maintain exclusion zones during testing.

\#### 6. Boiler, Pressure and Thermal Safety

Maintain approved water level, pressure relief, flame supervision, purge sequence, chemical treatment and inspection. Prevent contact with hot steam, surfaces and ash.

\#### 7. Fuel, Fire and Explosion Safety

Control ignition sources, leaks, dust, ventilation and static charge. Maintain detection, suppression, emergency shutdown and safe fuel-handling procedures.

\#### 8. Technology-Specific Hazards

\- Nuclear: radiation protection, containment and licensed procedures
\- Hydro: water release, confined spaces and intake hazards
\- Wind: work at height, blade movement and rescue planning
\- Solar PV: persistent DC Voltage, Arc risk and backfeed
\- Battery Storage: DC fault, thermal runaway and hazardous gas

\#### 9. Safe Return to Service

Inspect completed work, remove tools and temporary connections under procedure, restore guards, confirm personnel clearance, remove safety documents by authorization and conduct a controlled test.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

Before maintaining a Boiler Feed Pump, technicians must isolate its electrical supply and also secure steam, hot water, pressure, rotating parts and any automatic-start command.

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- A Permit to Work defines controlled work boundaries.
\- Isolation must cover electrical and non-electrical Energy.
\- Verify absence of Voltage; never assume isolation.
\- Each plant technology adds specific hazards.
\- Safe restoration is as important as safe shutdown.

\---

\## Topic 13: Practical Applications and Industrial Importance

\### Definition

The industrial importance of Power Generation is its role in providing reliable, quality electricity for manufacturing, infrastructure, commercial activity and essential public services.

\---

\### Importance

Industrial production depends not only on installed MW but also on continuity, Voltage, frequency, fault performance, restoration capability and predictable Energy cost.

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Industrial Production

Steel, cement, textile, chemical, food, pharmaceutical and manufacturing facilities require continuous Power for Motors, heating, cooling, automation and quality control.

\#### 2. Captive and Cogeneration Plants

Captive Generation serves a facility's own load. Combined Heat and Power uses fuel to produce electricity and useful process Heat, increasing total fuel utilization when Heat demand is suitable.

\#### 3. Critical Services

Hospitals, data centers, water systems, transportation, communication and emergency services use Grid supply with appropriate UPS, Battery or standby generation arrangements.

\#### 4. Generation Planning

Planning requires demand forecast, reserve margin, fuel/resource study, transmission assessment, environmental approval, finance, construction schedule and retirement plan.

\#### 5. Economic Dispatch

Available generating units are scheduled to meet demand and reserve at acceptable cost while respecting ramp, minimum-output, transmission, fuel and emission constraints.

\#### 6. Reliability and Resilience

Diverse fuel, geographically distributed plants, interconnection, storage, black-start resources, spare parts and trained personnel improve recovery from faults and disasters.

\#### 7. Career Relevance

Generation-sector roles include:

\- Plant operation and control-room engineering
\- Electrical, mechanical and Instrumentation maintenance
\- Protection, excitation and Generator testing
\- Energy audit and performance engineering
\- Solar/Wind design and commissioning
\- Grid integration, dispatch and planning
\- Safety, environmental and reliability engineering

\#### 8. Practical Selection Table

| Need | Suitable Direction | Main Check |
|---|---|---|
| Emergency backup | Diesel/Gas/Battery arrangement | Start time and autonomy |
| Variable daytime industrial load | Grid + Solar + controls/storage as justified | Load profile and Grid rules |
| Continuous process Heat and Power | Cogeneration | Heat-to-Power demand match |
| Remote community | Mini-grid with renewable, storage and backup | Resource and maintenance capability |
| National peak support | Fast Hydro, Gas Turbine, storage or demand response | Ramp, duration and transmission |

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

A textile factory may combine reliable Grid supply, rooftop Solar for daytime Energy reduction and an emergency Generator for critical processes, coordinated by protection and automatic transfer controls.

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Reliable generation enables industrial productivity and public services.
\- Captive Generation and Cogeneration serve specific facility needs.
\- Power quality and restoration matter as much as Energy quantity.
\- Planning integrates generation, fuel, transmission, environment and finance.
\- Generation knowledge supports many EEE technical careers.

\# Chapter Summary

\- Electrical Power Generation converts primary Energy into usable electricity.
\- Most large plants use a prime mover and Synchronous Generator; Solar PV uses direct electrical conversion and an inverter.
\- Conventional plants include Steam Thermal, Hydro, Nuclear, Diesel and Gas-Turbine stations.
\- Combined-Cycle generation recovers Gas-Turbine exhaust Heat to improve Efficiency.
\- Renewable plants include Solar, Wind, Biomass, Tidal and Geothermal systems.
\- Hydro Power is approximately P = ρgQHη.
\- Wind-resource Power is proportional to air density, swept area and the cube of wind speed.
\- Base Load is the continuous minimum demand; Peak Load occurs for shorter periods.
\- A Load Curve preserves time order; a Load Duration Curve sorts load from highest to lowest.
\- Area under either load curve represents Energy.
\- Load Factor equals Average Load divided by Maximum Demand.
\- Capacity Factor compares actual Energy with rated maximum possible Energy.
\- Plant Factor terminology varies, so the adopted definition must be stated.
\- Installed capacity is not the same as available Power or generated Energy.
\- Bangladesh uses gas, coal, liquid fuel, Hydro, Solar, Wind and imported Power within a mixed institutional structure.
\- Environmental assessment must include air, water, land, climate, waste and community effects.
\- Power-Plant safety requires controlled electrical, mechanical, thermal, pressure and process isolation.
\- Reliable generation is fundamental to industry, healthcare, water, communication and economic development.
