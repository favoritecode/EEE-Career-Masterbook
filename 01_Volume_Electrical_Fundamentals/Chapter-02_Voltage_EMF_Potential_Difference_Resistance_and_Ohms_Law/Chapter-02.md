\## Introduction



Voltage, Electromotive Force (EMF), Potential Difference, Resistance and Ohm's Law are fundamental concepts of Electrical and Electronic Engineering (EEE). These concepts explain why electric charge moves, how electrical sources supply energy and how circuit elements oppose electric current.



Every practical electrical circuit—from a battery-operated torch to an industrial motor control panel—depends on the relationship between Voltage, Current and Resistance.



This chapter explains these concepts in a simple, practical and engineering-oriented manner. It is intended for university students, diploma students, technical job preparation, competitive examinations and self-learning.

\## Topic 01: Voltage



\### Definition



Voltage is the electrical potential difference between two points. It represents the electrical energy transferred per unit charge when a charge moves between those points.



Mathematically,



\*\*V = W / Q\*\*



Where:



\- V = Voltage in Volt (V)

\- W = Electrical Work or Energy in Joule (J)

\- Q = Electric Charge in Coulomb (C)



Therefore,



\*\*1 Volt = 1 Joule / 1 Coulomb\*\*



\---



\### Importance



Voltage is important because it provides the electrical pressure that can drive current through a closed circuit. Without a voltage difference, there is no continuous transfer of electrical energy through a resistive load.



Voltage is essential for:



\- Operating electrical and electronic equipment.

\- Selecting the correct rating of cables and components.

\- Measuring the condition of batteries and power supplies.

\- Troubleshooting electrical circuits.

\- Understanding power generation, transmission and distribution.



\---



\### Main Concepts



Voltage is always measured between two points. A voltmeter or Digital Multimeter (DMM) is connected in parallel with the two points being measured.



Common voltage sources include:



\- Batteries

\- DC power supplies

\- Generators

\- Solar cells

\- Utility power systems



Voltage may be:



\#### 1. Direct Voltage (DC Voltage)



Direct Voltage maintains a fixed polarity. Batteries and regulated DC power supplies are common examples.



\#### 2. Alternating Voltage (AC Voltage)



Alternating Voltage changes magnitude and polarity periodically. Household and industrial power supplies are common examples.



\---



\### Real-Life Applications



\- A 1.5 V cell supplies a small torch circuit.

\- A 12 V battery operates automotive electrical systems.

\- A 24 V DC supply is commonly used in industrial control circuits.

\- Utility voltage supplies homes, offices and factories.

\- A technician measures voltage to identify a faulty power supply or open circuit.



\---



\### Key Takeaways



\- Voltage is electrical energy transferred per unit charge.

\- Symbol: V

\- SI Unit: Volt (V)

\- Voltage is measured between two points.

\- A Voltmeter is connected in parallel.

\## Topic 02: Electromotive Force (EMF)



\### Definition



Electromotive Force (EMF) is the energy supplied by an electrical source per unit charge as the source moves charge through itself from lower potential to higher potential.



Mathematically,



\*\*E = W / Q\*\*



Where:



\- E = EMF in Volt (V)

\- W = Energy supplied by the source in Joule (J)

\- Q = Electric Charge in Coulomb (C)



Despite its name, EMF is not a mechanical force. It is an energy-per-charge quantity measured in Volt.



\---



\### Importance



EMF describes the ability of a source to supply electrical energy. It is important for understanding batteries, generators, solar cells and other energy-conversion devices.



EMF helps engineers to:



\- Determine the no-load voltage of a source.

\- Select a suitable source for a circuit.

\- Understand voltage drop inside a practical source.

\- Analyze source performance under load.



\---



\### Main Concepts



EMF is produced when another form of energy is converted into electrical energy.



Examples include:



\- Chemical Energy → Electrical Energy in a battery

\- Mechanical Energy → Electrical Energy in a generator

\- Light Energy → Electrical Energy in a solar cell



For a practical source with internal resistance:



\*\*V = E − Ir\*\*



Where:



\- V = Terminal Voltage

\- E = EMF of the source

\- I = Load Current

\- r = Internal Resistance



When no current is drawn, the terminal voltage is approximately equal to the EMF.



\---



\### Real-Life Applications



\- Chemical reactions produce EMF in batteries.

\- Rotating generators produce EMF in power stations.

\- Solar panels produce EMF from sunlight.

\- Thermocouples produce a small EMF due to temperature difference.

\- Measuring open-circuit voltage helps estimate the condition of a source.



\---



\### Key Takeaways



\- EMF is energy supplied by a source per unit charge.

\- Symbol: E or ε

\- SI Unit: Volt (V)

\- EMF is not a mechanical force.

\- A practical source has internal resistance.

\## Topic 03: Potential Difference



\### Definition



Potential Difference is the electrical energy converted or transferred per unit charge when charge moves between two points in a circuit.



Mathematically,



\*\*V = W / Q\*\*



Potential Difference is commonly called Voltage and is measured in Volt (V).



\---



\### Importance



Potential Difference explains how electrical energy is transferred to circuit components. A resistor, lamp, motor or heater converts electrical energy when current passes through it and a potential difference exists across its terminals.



It is important for:



\- Calculating energy transfer in a load.

\- Measuring voltage drop across components.

\- Checking whether a component receives the required voltage.

\- Applying Kirchhoff's Voltage Law in circuit analysis.



\---



\### Main Concepts



Potential Difference exists between two points and is measured by placing a voltmeter in parallel.



The difference between EMF and Potential Difference can be summarized as follows:



| Property | EMF | Potential Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | Energy supplied per unit charge | Energy converted per unit charge |
| Common Location | Across an electrical source | Across a circuit component or two points |
| Symbol | E or ε | V |
| SI Unit | Volt (V) | Volt (V) |
| Typical Measurement | Open-circuit source voltage | Voltage across a load |



In practical circuits, the terminal voltage of a source may be lower than its EMF because of internal resistance.



\---



\### Real-Life Applications



\- A lamp converts electrical energy because a potential difference exists across it.

\- A voltage drop across a cable indicates energy loss in its resistance.

\- A technician measures potential difference across a fuse to check whether it is open.

\- Electronic circuits use specific potential differences to bias transistors and integrated circuits.



\---



\### Key Takeaways



\- Potential Difference is energy transferred per unit charge.

\- It is measured between two points.

\- Symbol: V

\- SI Unit: Volt (V)

\- EMF supplies energy; a load converts electrical energy.

\## Topic 04: Resistance



\### Definition



Electrical Resistance is the property of a material or component that opposes the flow of electric current.



For an ohmic conductor,



\*\*R = V / I\*\*



Where:



\- R = Resistance in Ohm (Ω)

\- V = Potential Difference in Volt (V)

\- I = Current in Ampere (A)



\---



\### Importance



Resistance controls current and converts electrical energy into other forms, especially heat. Correct resistance values are essential for safe and reliable circuit operation.



Resistance is important for:



\- Limiting current in electronic circuits.

\- Producing heat in electrical heaters.

\- Setting voltage and current levels.

\- Protecting components from excessive current.

\- Measuring the condition of wires, windings and circuit components.



\---



\### Main Concepts



Materials have different levels of resistance because their atomic structures affect the movement of charge carriers.



Low-resistance materials such as Copper and Aluminium are used as conductors. High-resistance materials such as Nichrome are used in heating elements.



Common resistance values are expressed using:



\- Milliohm (mΩ) = 10⁻³ Ω

\- Kilohm (kΩ) = 10³ Ω

\- Megohm (MΩ) = 10⁶ Ω



Resistance is measured using an Ohmmeter or Digital Multimeter. The circuit must be de-energized before a normal resistance measurement is made.



\---



\### Real-Life Applications



\- Resistors limit LED current.

\- Heating elements use high resistance to produce heat.

\- Motor windings have measurable resistance.

\- Cable resistance causes voltage drop and power loss.

\- Insulation resistance testing helps identify electrical safety problems.



\---



\### Key Takeaways



\- Resistance opposes electric current.

\- Symbol: R

\- SI Unit: Ohm (Ω)

\- An Ohmmeter measures resistance.

\- Resistance measurements are normally made on a de-energized circuit.

\## Topic 05: Factors Affecting Resistance



\### Definition



The resistance of a uniform conductor depends on its material, length, cross-sectional area and temperature.



At a specified temperature,



\*\*R = ρL / A\*\*



Where:



\- R = Resistance in Ohm (Ω)

\- ρ = Resistivity in Ohm-metre (Ω·m)

\- L = Length of the conductor in Metre (m)

\- A = Cross-sectional Area in Square Metre (m²)



\---



\### Importance



Understanding the factors affecting resistance helps engineers select cable sizes, design heating elements, estimate voltage drop and evaluate the effect of temperature on equipment.



Incorrect conductor selection may cause:



\- Excessive voltage drop.

\- Power loss.

\- Overheating.

\- Reduced equipment performance.

\- Fire and safety hazards.



\---



\### Main Concepts



\#### 1. Material



Different materials have different resistivities. Copper has low resistivity, while Nichrome has comparatively high resistivity.



\#### 2. Length



Resistance is directly proportional to conductor length.



\*\*R ∝ L\*\*



A longer conductor has greater resistance when material, area and temperature remain unchanged.



\#### 3. Cross-sectional Area



Resistance is inversely proportional to cross-sectional area.



\*\*R ∝ 1 / A\*\*



A thicker conductor has lower resistance when material, length and temperature remain unchanged.



\#### 4. Temperature



For most metallic conductors, resistance increases as temperature increases. For many semiconductor materials, resistance decreases as temperature increases.



For a metallic conductor over a limited temperature range:



\*\*R₂ = R₁[1 + α(T₂ − T₁)]\*\*



Where α is the temperature coefficient of resistance at the reference temperature.



\---



\### Real-Life Applications



\- Long cables require voltage-drop consideration.

\- Larger cable cross-sectional area reduces resistance and power loss.

\- Copper is selected for wiring because of its low resistivity.

\- Nichrome is used in heaters because of its higher resistivity and heat resistance.

\- Resistance Temperature Detectors (RTDs) use predictable resistance change with temperature.



\---



\### Key Takeaways



\- Resistance depends on material, length, area and temperature.

\- Longer conductor → Higher Resistance.

\- Larger area → Lower Resistance.

\- Resistivity is a property of the material.

\- Temperature affects metals and semiconductors differently.

\## Topic 06: Ohm's Law



\### Definition



Ohm's Law states that the current through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across it, provided that temperature and other physical conditions remain constant.



Mathematically,



\*\*V = IR\*\*



The formula can also be written as:



\*\*I = V / R\*\*



\*\*R = V / I\*\*



\---



\### Importance



Ohm's Law is one of the most frequently used relationships in electrical engineering. It connects Voltage, Current and Resistance and allows unknown circuit quantities to be calculated.



Ohm's Law is essential for:



\- Basic circuit calculation.

\- Selecting resistors and current-limiting components.

\- Troubleshooting electrical equipment.

\- Estimating current and voltage drop.

\- Understanding advanced circuit analysis.



\---



\### Main Concepts



For a fixed resistance:



\- Increasing Voltage increases Current.

\- Decreasing Voltage decreases Current.



For a fixed voltage:



\- Increasing Resistance decreases Current.

\- Decreasing Resistance increases Current.



Example:



If a 12 V source is connected across a 6 Ω resistor:



\*\*I = V / R\*\*



\*\*I = 12 / 6 = 2 A\*\*



An ohmic conductor has a straight-line V–I characteristic through the origin when temperature remains constant. Ohm's Law does not describe every device under all operating conditions. Diodes, filament lamps with changing temperature and many semiconductor devices are non-ohmic.



\---



\### Real-Life Applications



\- Calculating current through a resistor.

\- Selecting a resistor for an LED circuit.

\- Estimating voltage drop in cables.

\- Checking whether measured circuit values are reasonable.

\- Calculating the resistance of a heater or winding from measured voltage and current.



\---



\### Key Takeaways



\- Ohm's Law: V = IR

\- Voltage is directly proportional to current for constant resistance.

\- Temperature and physical conditions must remain constant.

\- Ohm's Law is applicable to ohmic conductors.

\- It is a fundamental tool for circuit calculation and troubleshooting.
