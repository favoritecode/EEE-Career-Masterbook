\# Magnetism and Electromagnetism

\## Introduction

Magnetism and Electromagnetism are fundamental parts of Electrical Engineering. They explain how magnets produce forces, how Electric Current creates a Magnetic Field and how a changing Magnetic Flux produces an induced Voltage.

These principles form the foundation of transformers, generators, motors, relays, solenoids, measuring instruments, loudspeakers, induction-heating systems and many other electrical devices.

This chapter introduces magnetic quantities, materials and laws in a simple, practical and engineering-oriented manner. It is suitable for BSc Engineering students, Diploma students, technical job preparation, competitive examinations and self-learning.

\- Describe Electromagnetic Induction.

\## Topic 01: Magnetism

\### Definition

Magnetism is a physical phenomenon associated with moving electric charges, magnetic materials and Magnetic Fields. It produces forces of attraction or repulsion between magnetic objects and can also exert force on moving electric charges and Current-carrying conductors.

A magnet has two poles:

\- North Pole (N)
\- South Pole (S)

The magnetic effect is strongest near these poles.

\---

\### Importance

Understanding Magnetism is essential because it helps engineers explain:

\- The operation of motors and generators
\- Transformer action
\- Electromagnetic relays and contactors
\- Magnetic measuring instruments
\- Data storage and sensing
\- Magnetic lifting and separation
\- The relationship between Electricity and Magnetism

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Magnetic Poles

Every magnet has a North Pole and a South Pole. The poles cannot normally be separated into isolated magnetic poles by cutting an ordinary magnet; each smaller piece forms a new magnet with both poles.

\#### 2. Law of Magnetic Poles

Magnetic poles follow two basic rules:

\- Like poles repel each other.
\- Unlike poles attract each other.

\#### 3. Permanent Magnet

A Permanent Magnet retains significant Magnetism after the magnetizing influence is removed.

Common Permanent Magnet materials include:

\- Hard ferrite
\- Alnico
\- Neodymium-Iron-Boron (NdFeB)
\- Samarium-Cobalt (SmCo)

\#### 4. Temporary Magnet

A Temporary Magnet shows strong Magnetism while an external Magnetic Field is present but loses most of it when the field is removed.

Soft Iron is commonly used where easy magnetization and demagnetization are required.

\#### 5. Magnetic and Non-Magnetic Behavior

Some materials respond strongly to a Magnetic Field, while others respond only weakly. Engineering classification requires more detail than simply calling a material magnetic or non-magnetic.

\#### 6. Magnetic Force

A Magnetic Field can exert force on:

\- Another magnet
\- A suitable Magnetic Material
\- A moving electric charge
\- A Current-carrying conductor

This force is the basis of electromagnetic motion and many sensing systems.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

Magnetism is used in:

\- Compass needles
\- Electric motors and generators
\- Loudspeakers and headphones
\- Magnetic door catches
\- Magnetic sensors
\- Computer storage devices
\- Magnetic separators
\- MRI systems

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Magnetism is associated with moving charges and Magnetic Fields.
\- Every ordinary magnet has a North Pole and a South Pole.
\- Like poles repel and unlike poles attract.
\- Permanent Magnets retain Magnetism, while Temporary Magnets lose most of it after the magnetizing influence is removed.
\- Magnetic Force is essential in electrical machines, instruments and sensors.

\## Topic 02: Magnetic Materials

\### Definition

Magnetic Materials are materials whose internal magnetic moments respond to an applied Magnetic Field. Their response depends on atomic structure, electron motion, magnetic-domain behavior and temperature.

For introductory Electrical Engineering, Magnetic Materials are commonly discussed as Diamagnetic, Paramagnetic and Ferromagnetic materials. Ferrimagnetic materials are also important in practical magnetic cores.

\---

\### Importance

Knowledge of Magnetic Materials helps engineers:

\- Select cores for transformers and inductors
\- Design motors, generators and electromagnets
\- Reduce magnetic-core losses
\- Select Permanent Magnet materials
\- Improve relay and solenoid performance
\- Understand magnetic shielding and sensing

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Diamagnetic Materials

Diamagnetic Materials develop a weak magnetic response opposite to the applied Magnetic Field and are weakly repelled.

Examples include:

\- Copper
\- Silver
\- Gold
\- Bismuth

Their relative Permeability is slightly less than one.

\#### 2. Paramagnetic Materials

Paramagnetic Materials are weakly attracted by an applied Magnetic Field. Their relative Permeability is slightly greater than one.

Examples include:

\- Aluminium
\- Platinum
\- Magnesium

They do not normally retain strong Magnetism after the external field is removed.

\#### 3. Ferromagnetic Materials

Ferromagnetic Materials can be strongly magnetized because many atomic magnetic moments align in regions called Magnetic Domains.

Examples include:

\- Iron
\- Cobalt
\- Nickel
\- Some of their alloys

Their Permeability can be much greater than that of free space.

\#### 4. Ferrimagnetic Materials

Ferrimagnetic Materials contain opposing magnetic moments of unequal magnitude, producing a net magnetic effect. Ferrites are widely used in high-frequency transformer and inductor cores because of their high electrical Resistivity and relatively low Eddy-Current Loss.

\#### 5. Soft Magnetic Materials

Soft Magnetic Materials are easy to magnetize and demagnetize. They normally have high Permeability, low Coercivity and a narrow Hysteresis Loop.

Applications include:

\- Transformer cores
\- Inductor cores
\- Relay cores
\- Electromagnets

\#### 6. Hard Magnetic Materials

Hard Magnetic Materials resist demagnetization. They normally have high Coercivity and are suitable for Permanent Magnets.

\#### 7. Curie Temperature

Above its Curie Temperature, a ferromagnetic material loses its ferromagnetic ordering and becomes paramagnetic. The Curie Temperature depends on the material.

\#### 8. Comparison of Magnetic Materials

| Material Class | Response to Applied Field | Relative Permeability | Typical Examples |
|----------------|---------------------------|-----------------------|------------------|
| Diamagnetic | Weakly repelled | Slightly less than 1 | Copper, Bismuth |
| Paramagnetic | Weakly attracted | Slightly greater than 1 | Aluminium, Platinum |
| Ferromagnetic | Strongly attracted and magnetized | Much greater than 1 | Iron, Cobalt, Nickel |
| Ferrimagnetic | Strong net magnetic response | Usually greater than 1 | Ferrites |

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

Magnetic Materials are used in:

\- Transformer and inductor cores
\- Motor and generator magnetic circuits
\- Permanent Magnet machines
\- Relays, contactors and solenoids
\- Magnetic recording and memory
\- Electromagnetic shielding
\- High-frequency power supplies
\- Magnetic sensors

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Magnetic Materials respond differently to an applied Magnetic Field.
\- Diamagnetic Materials are weakly repelled.
\- Paramagnetic Materials are weakly attracted.
\- Ferromagnetic Materials can be strongly magnetized.
\- Soft Magnetic Materials are suitable for repeatedly changing fields.
\- Hard Magnetic Materials are suitable for Permanent Magnets.
\- Ferrites are useful at high frequency because of their high electrical Resistivity.

\## Topic 03: Magnetic Field

\### Definition

A Magnetic Field is a region in which a magnetic force can act on a magnet, a Magnetic Material, a moving electric charge or a Current-carrying conductor.

The Magnetic Field is represented by the vector quantity Magnetic Flux Density, symbol \*\*B\*\*. Its SI unit is Tesla (T).

\---

\### Importance

Understanding the Magnetic Field is necessary for:

\- Electrical machine analysis
\- Transformer and inductor design
\- Electromagnetic force calculation
\- Magnetic sensing
\- Induction and energy-conversion systems
\- Field visualization and magnetic-circuit design

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Magnetic Field Lines

Magnetic Field Lines are an imaginary visual representation of a Magnetic Field. Outside a magnet, their conventional direction is from the North Pole to the South Pole. Inside the magnet, they continue from the South Pole to the North Pole, forming closed paths.

\#### 2. Properties of Magnetic Field Lines

\- They form continuous closed loops.
\- They do not intersect one another.
\- Their tangent indicates the local field direction.
\- Greater line density represents a stronger field in a qualitative diagram.

\#### 3. Magnetic Flux Density

Magnetic Flux Density indicates the concentration and direction of Magnetic Flux in a region.

For a uniform field normal to an area:

\*\*B = Φ/A\*\*

Where:

\- B = Magnetic Flux Density in Tesla (T)
\- Φ = Magnetic Flux in Weber (Wb)
\- A = Area in square metres (m²)

In the general uniform-field case:

\*\*Φ = BA cos θ\*\*

where θ is the angle between the Magnetic Field and the normal to the surface.

\#### 4. Magnetic Field Strength

Magnetic Field Strength is represented by \*\*H\*\* and measured in ampere per metre (A/m).

For a long ideal solenoid:

\*\*H ≈ NI/l\*\*

Where N is the number of turns, I is Current and l is the magnetic-path length.

\#### 5. Relationship Between B and H

For a linear isotropic medium:

\*\*B = μH\*\*

Where μ is the Permeability of the medium. Ferromagnetic materials are generally nonlinear, so μ may vary with operating point.

\#### 6. Field Around a Straight Conductor

A Current-carrying straight conductor produces circular Magnetic Field Lines around it. The Right-Hand Grip Rule determines the direction:

\- Thumb points in the direction of conventional Current.
\- Curled fingers show the Magnetic Field direction.

\#### 7. Field of a Coil

A Current-carrying coil produces a field similar to that of a bar magnet. Increasing the Current, number of turns or core Permeability generally increases the magnetic effect.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

Magnetic Fields are important in:

\- Motors and generators
\- Transformers and inductors
\- Hall-effect sensors
\- Magnetic compasses
\- Loudspeakers
\- Magnetic levitation
\- Current sensors
\- Medical imaging systems

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- A Magnetic Field is a region of magnetic influence.
\- Magnetic Field Lines form closed loops and never intersect.
\- Magnetic Flux Density is represented by B and measured in Tesla.
\- Magnetic Field Strength is represented by H and measured in A/m.
\- For a linear medium, B = μH.
\- Electric Current produces a Magnetic Field.

\## Topic 04: Magnetic Flux

\### Definition

Magnetic Flux is a measure of the total Magnetic Field passing through a selected surface. It is represented by the symbol \*\*Φ\*\* and its SI unit is Weber (Wb).

For a uniform Magnetic Field:

\*\*Φ = BA cos θ\*\*

Where θ is the angle between the Magnetic Field and the normal to the surface.

\---

\### Importance

Magnetic Flux is important because it connects the Magnetic Field with:

\- Induced EMF
\- Transformer action
\- Generator operation
\- Inductor behavior
\- Magnetic-circuit calculations
\- Electromechanical energy conversion

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Unit of Magnetic Flux

The SI unit of Magnetic Flux is Weber (Wb).

\*\*1 Wb = 1 T·m²\*\*

A change of Flux Linkage at one Weber-turn per second induces one Volt.

\#### 2. Magnetic Flux Density

For a uniform field perpendicular to a surface:

\*\*B = Φ/A\*\*

Therefore, one Tesla equals one Weber per square metre:

\*\*1 T = 1 Wb/m²\*\*

\#### 3. Flux Linkage

If the same Magnetic Flux links all N turns of a coil, the Flux Linkage is:

\*\*λ = NΦ\*\*

Where λ is measured in Weber-turns (Wb-turn).

\#### 4. Effect of Surface Orientation

Flux is maximum when the field is normal to the surface (θ = 0°) and ideally zero when the field is parallel to the surface (θ = 90°).

\#### 5. Magnetic Circuit Analogy

In a simplified magnetic circuit:

\*\*Φ = MMF/ℜ\*\*

Where:

\- MMF = Magnetomotive Force in ampere-turns (At)
\- ℜ = Reluctance in ampere-turns per Weber (At/Wb)

This relationship is analogous in form to Current = Voltage/Resistance, but magnetic and electrical circuits are not physically identical.

\#### 6. Leakage Flux

Not all Flux produced by a coil follows the intended core path or links every winding. The portion outside the intended path is called Leakage Flux.

\#### 7. Fringing

At an air gap, Magnetic Flux spreads outward instead of remaining completely confined to the core cross-section. This effect is called Fringing.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

Magnetic Flux is used in the analysis of:

\- Transformer cores
\- Generators and alternators
\- Electric motors
\- Inductors and chokes
\- Relays and solenoids
\- Magnetic sensors
\- Wireless power-transfer systems
\- Electromagnetic actuators

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Magnetic Flux is represented by Φ and measured in Weber.
\- For a uniform field, Φ = BA cos θ.
\- Magnetic Flux Density is Flux per unit normal area.
\- Flux Linkage equals NΦ when the same Flux links every turn.
\- Leakage Flux does not follow the complete intended magnetic path.
\- Changing Flux Linkage is the basis of Electromagnetic Induction.

\## Topic 05: Electromagnetism

\### Definition

Electromagnetism describes the relationship between Electricity and Magnetism. Electric charges produce electric effects, and moving charges or Electric Current produce a Magnetic Field.

An Electromagnet is commonly formed by passing Current through a coil, often wound around a Soft Magnetic core to strengthen and guide the Magnetic Field.

\---

\### Importance

Electromagnetism is essential for:

\- Converting electrical energy into mechanical motion
\- Producing electrical switching and control action
\- Creating controllable Magnetic Fields
\- Operating motors, relays, contactors and solenoids
\- Transferring energy through magnetic coupling
\- Understanding generators and transformers

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Magnetic Effect of Electric Current

When Electric Current flows through a conductor, a Magnetic Field forms around the conductor. Reversing the Current reverses the field direction.

\#### 2. Electromagnetic Coil

When a conductor is wound into a coil, the fields produced by individual turns reinforce one another. A solenoid produces a strong, directed field along its axis.

\#### 3. Factors Affecting Electromagnet Strength

The strength of an Electromagnet generally increases with:

\- Higher coil Current
\- More turns
\- A suitable high-Permeability core
\- A shorter and well-designed magnetic path
\- A smaller air gap, where mechanically appropriate

The coil and core must remain within their electrical and thermal ratings.

\#### 4. Polarity of an Electromagnet

The Right-Hand Grip Rule can identify coil polarity. Curl the fingers of the right hand in the direction of conventional Current around the turns; the thumb points toward the coil's North Pole.

\#### 5. Magnetomotive Force

The Magnetomotive Force produced by a coil is:

\*\*MMF = NI\*\*

Where:

\- N = Number of turns
\- I = Current in ampere

The practical unit is ampere-turn (At).

\#### 6. Electromagnetic Force

A Current-carrying conductor in an external Magnetic Field can experience a force. For a straight conductor under ideal perpendicular conditions:

\*\*F = BIl\*\*

In the general case:

\*\*F = BIl sin θ\*\*

This principle produces torque in electric motors.

\#### 7. Energy and Heating

An electromagnet coil consumes electrical Power and can heat because of winding Resistance. Core losses may also occur under changing fields. Continuous-duty and intermittent-duty coils must be used according to their ratings.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

Electromagnetism is used in:

\- Relays and contactors
\- Solenoid valves
\- Electric bells
\- Electromagnetic locks
\- Motor windings
\- Magnetic lifting cranes
\- Circuit breakers
\- Loudspeakers

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Electric Current produces a Magnetic Field.
\- A coil concentrates and strengthens the magnetic effect.
\- A Soft Magnetic core can significantly increase Flux.
\- Electromagnet strength depends on Current, turns and magnetic-path properties.
\- MMF = NI.
\- Electromagnetic Force is the basis of motors, relays and actuators.
\- Coil voltage, Current, temperature and duty rating must be respected.

\---

\## Topic 06: Electromagnetic Induction

\### Definition

Electromagnetic Induction is the production of an induced EMF in a conductor or circuit when the Magnetic Flux Linkage associated with it changes.

If the circuit is closed, the induced EMF can drive an induced Current. If the circuit is open, an induced Voltage may exist without a continuous Current.

\---

\### Importance

Electromagnetic Induction is the operating principle of:

\- Generators and alternators
\- Transformers
\- Induction motors
\- Inductive sensors
\- Wireless charging systems
\- Dynamic microphones
\- Eddy-Current devices

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Requirement for Induction

An induced EMF requires a change in Flux Linkage. Merely placing a stationary coil in a steady Magnetic Field does not induce a continuous EMF.

\#### 2. Methods of Changing Flux Linkage

Flux Linkage can change by:

\- Moving a conductor relative to a Magnetic Field
\- Moving a magnet relative to a coil
\- Changing the field strength
\- Changing coil area
\- Changing the angle between the field and coil
\- Changing Current in a nearby magnetically coupled coil

\#### 3. Dynamically Induced EMF

When a conductor physically cuts Magnetic Flux, a motional EMF is produced. For a straight conductor moving under ideal perpendicular conditions:

\*\*e = Blv\*\*

In the general case:

\*\*e = Blv sin θ\*\*

Where B is Flux Density, l is active conductor length and v is velocity.

\#### 4. Statically Induced EMF

A stationary coil can have an induced EMF when its linked Magnetic Flux changes with time. This occurs in transformers and inductors.

\#### 5. Self-Induction

When changing Current in a coil changes its own Flux Linkage and induces EMF in the same coil, the effect is called Self-Induction.

For an ideal linear inductor:

\*\*e = −L(di/dt)\*\*

\#### 6. Mutual Induction

When changing Current in one coil produces changing Flux that induces EMF in another coil, the effect is called Mutual Induction.

For ideal linear coupling notation:

\*\*e₂ = −M(di₁/dt)\*\*

\#### 7. Induced Current

Induced Current depends on the induced EMF and the total circuit Impedance. A larger induced EMF does not always mean a proportionally larger Current if Impedance also changes.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

Electromagnetic Induction is used in:

\- Electric power generation
\- Voltage transformation
\- Induction heating
\- Wireless charging
\- Bicycle dynamos
\- Metal detectors
\- Inductive proximity sensors
\- Dynamic microphones

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Electromagnetic Induction requires changing Flux Linkage.
\- An open circuit may have induced EMF without continuous induced Current.
\- Relative motion is one method of changing Flux Linkage.
\- A time-varying field can induce EMF in a stationary coil.
\- Self-Induction occurs in the same coil.
\- Mutual Induction occurs between magnetically coupled coils.

\## Topic 07: Faraday's Laws of Electromagnetic Induction

\### Definition

Faraday's Laws describe the production and magnitude of induced EMF caused by changing Magnetic Flux Linkage.

The general law for a coil is:

\*\*e = −d(NΦ)/dt\*\*

If the same Flux links all N turns:

\*\*e = −N(dΦ/dt)\*\*

The negative sign represents the direction given by Lenz's Law.

\---

\### Importance

Faraday's Laws allow engineers to:

\- Calculate induced EMF
\- Explain generator action
\- Analyze transformers and inductors
\- Design magnetic sensors
\- Understand the effect of frequency and Flux change
\- Predict induced Voltage during switching

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Faraday's First Law

Whenever the Magnetic Flux Linkage of a circuit changes, an EMF is induced in the circuit.

If the circuit is closed, the induced EMF may produce an induced Current.

\#### 2. Faraday's Second Law

The magnitude of induced EMF is equal to the time rate of change of Flux Linkage.

For average EMF over a finite interval:

\*\*|eavg| = N|ΔΦ/Δt|\*\*

when the same Flux links all turns.

\#### 3. Flux Linkage

Flux Linkage is represented by:

\*\*λ = NΦ\*\*

Therefore:

\*\*e = −dλ/dt\*\*

\#### 4. Factors Affecting Induced EMF

The magnitude of induced EMF increases with:

\- More coil turns
\- Faster change of Magnetic Flux
\- Stronger effective Magnetic Field
\- Greater active area or improved coupling, where these increase Flux Linkage
\- Higher relative speed in a generator arrangement

\#### 5. Numerical Example

A 200-turn coil experiences a uniform Flux change from 5 mWb to 1 mWb in 0.02 s.

Magnitude of average induced EMF:

\*\*|eavg| = N|ΔΦ|/Δt\*\*

\*\*= 200 × |1 − 5| × 10⁻³ / 0.02\*\*

\*\*= 40 V\*\*

The polarity must be determined using Lenz's Law and the selected reference direction.

\#### 6. Instantaneous and Average EMF

The derivative form gives instantaneous EMF. The finite-change form gives average EMF over a stated time interval.

\#### 7. Generator Action

In a generator, mechanical motion continually changes the Flux Linkage of conductors or coils, producing an induced EMF.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

Faraday's Laws are applied in:

\- Generator Voltage calculation
\- Transformer winding design
\- Inductor switching analysis
\- Speed and position sensing
\- Current transformers
\- Wireless power systems
\- Electromagnetic flow meters
\- Induction heating

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Faraday's First Law identifies when EMF is induced.
\- Faraday's Second Law determines the magnitude of induced EMF.
\- Induced EMF depends on the rate of change of Flux Linkage.
\- For a coil, e = −d(NΦ)/dt.
\- More turns or a faster Flux change generally produces greater induced EMF.
\- The negative sign represents Lenz's Law.

\## Topic 08: Lenz's Law

\### Definition

Lenz's Law states that the direction of an induced EMF and the resulting induced Current is such that the Magnetic Field produced by the induced Current opposes the change in Magnetic Flux that produced it.

Lenz's Law determines direction, while Faraday's Law determines the magnitude of induced EMF.

\---

\### Importance

Lenz's Law is important because it:

\- Determines induced Current direction
\- Expresses conservation of energy in induction
\- Explains counter EMF in motors
\- Explains electromagnetic braking and damping
\- Helps determine transformer and inductor polarity
\- Prevents physically impossible self-reinforcing induction predictions

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Opposition to Change

The induced effect opposes the change in Flux, not necessarily the original Magnetic Field itself.

If Flux through a coil increases in one direction, the induced Current creates a field opposing that increase. If the Flux decreases, the induced Current creates a field tending to maintain it.

\#### 2. Magnet Approaching a Coil

When a North Pole approaches a coil, the Flux through the coil increases. The near face of the coil develops an effective North Pole to oppose the approach.

\#### 3. Magnet Moving Away from a Coil

When the North Pole moves away, the Flux decreases. The near face of the coil develops an effective South Pole to oppose the decrease by attracting the receding magnet.

\#### 4. Negative Sign in Faraday's Law

The negative sign in:

\*\*e = −dλ/dt\*\*

represents the Lenz's Law polarity relative to the selected reference directions.

\#### 5. Direction-Finding Procedure

1\. Identify the direction of the original Magnetic Flux.
2\. Decide whether the Flux is increasing or decreasing.
3\. Select the induced field direction that opposes the change.
4\. Use the Right-Hand Grip Rule to determine induced Current direction.

\#### 6. Conservation of Energy

If the induced effect assisted the Flux change instead of opposing it, the system could produce increasing energy without an external input. Lenz's Law ensures that mechanical or electrical input energy is required.

\#### 7. Eddy Currents

Changing Flux can induce circulating Currents in bulk conductors. These Eddy Currents produce fields that oppose the change and can create braking force and heating.

Eddy Currents can be useful or undesirable depending on the application.

\---

\### Real-Life Applications

Lenz's Law explains:

\- Counter EMF in motors
\- Generator reaction torque
\- Eddy-Current brakes
\- Induction heating
\- Damping in measuring instruments
\- Metal detectors
\- Magnetic speed control
\- Falling-magnet demonstrations in conductive tubes

\---

\### Key Takeaways

\- Lenz's Law determines the direction of induced EMF and Current.
\- The induced Magnetic Field opposes the change in Flux that caused it.
\- It does not always oppose the original field; it opposes its change.
\- The negative sign in Faraday's Law represents Lenz's Law.
\- Lenz's Law is consistent with conservation of energy.
\- Eddy Currents can produce braking, damping and heating.

\## Topic 09: Practical Applications of Electromagnetism

\### Definition

Practical Applications of Electromagnetism are systems that use the relationship between Electric Current, Magnetic Fields, magnetic force and changing Flux Linkage to perform useful electrical, mechanical, control, sensing or energy-conversion functions.

\---

\### Importance

Electromagnetism is central to modern engineering because it enables:

\- Electrical energy generation
\- Voltage and Current transformation
\- Electrical-to-mechanical energy conversion
\- Automatic switching and actuation
\- Contactless sensing and power transfer
\- Audio conversion
\- Industrial heating and braking

\---

\### Main Concepts

\#### 1. Electric Motors

Motors use force on Current-carrying conductors in a Magnetic Field to produce torque and convert electrical energy into mechanical energy.

\#### 2. Generators

Generators use mechanical motion to change Flux Linkage and induce EMF, converting mechanical energy into electrical energy.

\#### 3. Transformers

Transformers use Mutual Induction between windings to transfer AC electrical energy at the same frequency while changing Voltage and Current levels according to design.

A transformer requires changing Flux and therefore does not provide normal continuous transformer action from steady DC.

\#### 4. Relays and Contactors

An energized coil creates magnetic force that moves an armature and changes electrical contacts. Relays and contactors allow a control circuit to operate another circuit.

\#### 5. Solenoids

A solenoid converts electrical input into linear mechanical motion. It is used in valves, locks, starters and automation mechanisms.

\#### 6. Loudspeakers and Microphones

A loudspeaker uses electromagnetic force to move a diaphragm and convert an electrical signal into sound. A dynamic microphone uses Electromagnetic Induction to convert diaphragm motion into an electrical signal.

\#### 7. Induction Heating

An alternating Magnetic Field induces Eddy Currents in a conductive workpiece. Resistive losses produce heat inside the material.

\#### 8. Electromagnetic Braking

Eddy Currents create a force opposing motion, producing contactless braking or damping.

\#### 9. Inductive Sensors

Inductive proximity sensors detect conductive objects by changes in an electromagnetic field. Current transformers and Rogowski coils use induction principles for Current measurement.

\#### 10. Wireless Power Transfer

Wireless charging systems transfer energy through changing magnetic coupling between transmitting and receiving coils. Alignment, frequency, coupling and control affect efficiency and safety.

\#### 11. Practical Safety

Electromagnetic devices may involve:

\- High Current and coil heating
\- High induced Voltage during switching
\- Mechanical movement and stored energy
\- Strong Magnetic Fields
\- Pinch and lifting hazards
\- Electromagnetic interference

Correct ratings, protective devices, insulation, grounding, safe isolation and manufacturer instructions must be followed.

\# Chapter Summary

\- Magnetism is associated with moving electric charges, magnetic moments and Magnetic Fields.
\- Every ordinary magnet has a North Pole and a South Pole.
\- Magnetic Materials include Diamagnetic, Paramagnetic, Ferromagnetic and Ferrimagnetic classes.
\- Soft Magnetic Materials are suitable for changing fields, while Hard Magnetic Materials are suitable for Permanent Magnets.
\- Magnetic Flux Density B is measured in Tesla, and Magnetic Field Strength H is measured in A/m.
\- Magnetic Flux Φ is measured in Weber.
\- For a uniform field, Φ = BA cos θ.
\- Electric Current produces a Magnetic Field, and a coil concentrates the magnetic effect.
\- Electromagnetic Induction occurs when Flux Linkage changes.
\- Faraday's Laws determine when EMF is induced and its magnitude.
\- Lenz's Law determines the direction of induced EMF and Current.
\- Self-Induction occurs in one coil, while Mutual Induction occurs between coupled coils.
\- Motors, generators, transformers, relays, solenoids and sensors depend on electromagnetic principles.
